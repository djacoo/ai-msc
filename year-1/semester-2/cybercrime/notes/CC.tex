\chapter{Crime in the Digital Society}
% ========================================

\section{Digitalisation: Definition and Short History}

\subsection{Digital Data}

\begin{definition}[Digital data]
Electronic representation of information in a format or language that computers can read and understand. In more technical terms: information in binary format (0--1) that is converted into a digital format readable by a computer.
\end{definition}

\subsection{A Brief History of Digitalisation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{1663}: John Graunt applies statistical analysis to mortality data from the bubonic plague --- an early precursor to data-driven reasoning.
    \item \textbf{1865}: R. Millar Devens coins the concept of business intelligence.
    \item \textbf{1884}: Herman Hollerith builds the tabulating machine for the US Census; later founds what becomes IBM.
    \item \textbf{1943}: Colossus, the first programmable electronic computer, is used to decipher Nazi communications at Bletchley Park.
    \item \textbf{1989}: Tim Berners-Lee creates the World Wide Web.
    \item \textbf{1995}: First supercomputer.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{From Digital Data to Big Data}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Since 1997}: Emergence of social networks.
    \item \textbf{1999}: The term \textbf{Internet of Things (IoT)} enters use --- the extension of the Internet to the world of physical objects.
    \item \textbf{2005}: Roger Mougalas coins the term \textbf{Big Data}: a set of digital data so large that it cannot be analysed and stored using traditional tools; specific technologies and analytical methods are needed to extract value from it.
\end{itemize}


\section{Case Study: Smart Toys and Criminal Risks}

This case study illustrates how IoT consumer devices --- specifically smart toys --- introduce concrete criminal risks by collecting and transmitting sensitive data about children.

\subsection{The Cayla Doll}

The Cayla doll was an internet-connected toy capable of recording and transmitting children's voice data. In 2017, Germany banned the device over fears that hackers could exploit it to target children. The case raises a fundamental question that recurs throughout the course: \textit{unfounded fears or documented reality?}

\subsection{CloudPets}

CloudPets were internet-connected stuffed animals that allowed children and parents to exchange voice messages via a companion app. A security analysis by Troy Hunt revealed that the backend database --- containing millions of voice recordings and user credentials --- was left exposed on the internet without authentication, was subsequently accessed by unauthorised parties, and the data was held for ransom. This is a documented case of a data breach compounded by ransom, affecting a particularly vulnerable population.

\subsection{Wiggy}

Wiggy was a crowdfunded ``smart piggy bank'' that linked to a child's savings account via a mobile app, allowing parents and relatives to deposit money remotely. The device combined financial account access with an IoT object marketed at children, creating a direct bridge between physical play and banking infrastructure --- a surface area for financial fraud and account compromise.


\section{Crime in the Digital Society}

The digital environment does not create crime \textit{ex novo}, but it transforms its scale, speed, authorship, and victim profile. The following dimensions illustrate this transformation through concrete examples.

\subsection{Crime Types}

Digital infrastructure enables new crime typologies or drastically scales existing ones:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cyberattacks on critical infrastructure}: In February 2023, a pro-Russian hacker group targeted Italian government websites and corporate portals (Carabinieri, Bper bank, A2A, TIM) coinciding with Prime Minister Meloni's visit to Kyiv. The Italian National Cybersecurity Agency (ACN) managed to mitigate the effects.
    \item \textbf{Election manipulation}: Investigative reporting identified a network led by ``Tal Hanan'' (former Israeli special forces operative) that allegedly manipulated 30 elections worldwide by deploying hacking, disinformation, and social engineering campaigns on behalf of paying clients.
    \item \textbf{Online fraud and sextortion}: Italian police data and news reports document a continuous rise in online fraud, with sextortion and fake social profiles representing a significant and growing share.
    \item \textbf{AI-assisted fraud}: In February 2025, a fraud scheme used AI-generated voice cloning to impersonate Italian Defence Minister Crosetto and a cloned ministerial phone number to solicit money from major Italian entrepreneurs (Armani, Moratti, Tronchetti Provera, Della Valle) under the pretext of ``freeing hostages''. At least one victim paid.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Crime Authors}

The digital environment radically diversifies who can commit crime and at what scale:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Individual actors}: Cody Wilson (b.\@ 1988), founder of Defense Distributed, published open-source gun designs (``wiki weapons'') for 3D printing online, gaining international notoriety in 2013 with the Liberator --- the first widely available 3D-printed pistol.
    \item \textbf{Teenage actors}: Graham Ivan Clark, at age 17, hacked Twitter's internal admin tool in 2020 and took control of high-profile accounts (Obama, Gates, Musk, West) to run a Bitcoin scam that defrauded victims of over \$100,000. He pleaded guilty to a three-year sentence.
    \item \textbf{Nation-state actors}: Bureau 121, North Korea's internal hacking unit, has been linked to several major malware events, most notably the \textbf{WannaCry ransomware attack} (2017), which infected around 300,000 devices across 150 countries and caused an estimated \$4 billion in damages. The US sanctioned North Korea over WannaCry in 2019.
    \item \textbf{AI systems as unintended actors}: Microsoft's Tay chatbot (2016) was corrupted by coordinated users within 24 hours of launch and began producing racist and misanthropic content at scale --- a case where the crime author is ambiguous and the platform's own technology becomes the vector.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Crime Victims}

Victimisation in the digital society is broader, faster, and less visible than in traditional crime:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sextortion}: FBI data documents a troubling increase in sextortion cases targeting minors. In one case (May 2022), a 17-year-old died by suicide hours after being scammed.
    \item \textbf{Mass data breach}: The Ashley Madison hack exposed the personal data of 32 million users of an extramarital affairs platform, with ongoing blackmail and reputational damage for victims years after the breach.
    \item \textbf{Cyber kidnapping}: A new criminal trend targeting foreign exchange students and their families. Police found a Chinese exchange student isolated and frightened in a tent in the Utah mountains; the perpetrators had convinced the victim to stage a kidnapping while extorting the family remotely.
    \item \textbf{Digital-enabled physical crime}: Thieves monitored Facebook posts to identify when homes were empty before burglarising them. In the Kim Kardashian Paris hotel robbery (2016), the perpetrators reportedly used her social media to track her location and movements.
    \item \textbf{Digital humiliation}: In Milan (2023), a perpetrator filmed and shared on social media footage of a physical assault she committed, leading victims' counsel to demand a public apology on TikTok as part of the settlement.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Social Control and Policing}

Digital technologies also reshape the instruments of social control:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Facial recognition}: In 2020, the European Commission considered a five-year ban on facial recognition in public spaces, highlighting the tension between law enforcement utility and civil liberties.
    \item \textbf{Algorithmic risk assessment}: Police forces increasingly use algorithmic tools to assess threat levels and predict criminal behaviour. A 2016 ProPublica investigation (``Machine Bias'') documented that the COMPAS recidivism prediction software used across US courts exhibited systematic racial bias, falsely flagging Black defendants as future criminals at nearly twice the rate of white defendants.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keypoint}
A recurring tension throughout the course: the same digital tools that enable crime also constitute the primary instruments of crime detection and social control. This duality --- and its legal, ethical, and criminological implications --- is the central analytical lens of the course.
\end{keypoint}

\section{Confessions of a Migrant Smuggler}

An ethnographic study by Andrea Di Nicola and Giampaolo Musumeci into migrant smuggling as a classic organised crime activity with first elements of digitalisation. The research was conducted in the years before the Mediterranean migration crisis and consisted of a two-year journey inside the minds of human smugglers, presenting the downside of the coin and debunking stereotypical ideas about ``scafisti''.

\subsection{First Elements of Digitalisation}

Smugglers were found to use technology and digital tools for several purposes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item For organisational purposes and to conduct their activities.
    \item To communicate with other smugglers in the network.
    \item To recruit clients and market their services.
    \item To acquire the tools necessary for the business (e.g.\ falsified documents, credit card information).
\end{itemize}

New means of communication adopted by smuggling networks include Skype, satellite phones and cellphones, wireless networks, email, and instant messaging apps.


\section{Project Surf and Sound: The Digitalisation of Migrant Smuggling}

The Surf and Sound project aimed at ``improving and sharing knowledge on the Internet's role in the human trafficking process''. It was the first research project to produce systematic knowledge on the use of the Internet (including social networks) in trafficking in human beings (THB) and migrant smuggling, disseminating findings through workshops for law enforcement agencies (LEAs) and NGOs. The project was co-financed by the European Commission --- DG Home Affairs within the ISEC 2013 ``Prevention and Fight against Crime -- Trafficking in Human Beings'' programme.

\subsection{Methodology}

The study employed a mixed-methods design:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Internet exploration (virtual ethnography)}: passive and active virtual ethnography ($N = 939$).
    \item \textbf{In-depth interviews} ($N = 58$): LEAs (16), NGOs (13), victims/clients (14), traffickers/smugglers (13), and cybercrime experts (2).
    \item \textbf{Online web survey} ($N = 995$): analysing the experience of internet users with web content linked to human trafficking and people smuggling.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Key Results}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The Internet plays a relevant role in the smuggling of migrants.
    \item Content linked to migrant smuggling services on social media websites is easily accessible.
    \item Social media websites facilitate the advertisement of smuggling services; Facebook and Instagram are more used than Twitter.
    \item Social media pages and profiles run by smugglers provide information on travels.
    \item Social media profiles and pages run by smugglers are very effective and responsive.
    \item Smugglers provide potential migrants with detailed information via mobile applications.
    \item Social media websites play a key role in the decision-making process of potential migrants.
    \item Social media websites are used by migrants to post feedback about smuggling services.
    \item The Internet is used also during and after the journey.
    \item The deep/dark web seems to play a marginal role.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Project FAKECARE: The Digitalisation of the Illegal Trade of Medicines}

\subsection{The Problem}

An estimated \textbf{10\%} of medicines worldwide are fake, illegal, or substandard. There are significant geographical differences: in developed countries the figure is below 1\%, while in developing countries it ranges from 15--30\% (sometimes reaching 50\%). This results in an estimated 100,000 to 700,000 deaths annually, with a global estimated turnover of 75 billion USD per year (\textbf{10 billion EUR/year in the EU}).

\subsection{Falsified Medicinal Products}

The use of several terms (``counterfeit'', ``falsified'', ``false'', ``tampered'') creates great confusion. For clarity, falsified medicinal products are \textbf{medicines that are dangerous} for private and public health. The umbrella category of \textbf{illegally traded (or produced) medicines} (ITMs) includes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Falsified branded medicines
    \item Falsified generic medicines
    \item Illegal medicines
    \item Medicines without authorisation
    \item Medicines with expired authorisation
    \item Legal (and genuine) medicines sold without a valid prescription
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Methodology}

\paragraph{Quantitative methods.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Web survey}: profiling the risk for customers to buy a falsified medicinal product online.
    \item \textbf{Honey-pot websites}: two fake online pharmacy websites set up to attract customers, automatically monitor their behaviour, and calculate statistics on visitors, clicks, and checkouts.
    \item \textbf{Quantitative content analysis}: on online pharmacies, customers' reviews on illegal online pharmacies, and news from the press.
    \item \textbf{Statistics} on the online trade of falsified medicines using data provided by regulatory agencies, law enforcement agencies, and Interpol.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Qualitative methods.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Analysis of \textbf{EU regulations} in the field of the online trade of falsified medicines.
    \item \textbf{Virtual ethnography}: exploring online forums and communities (including social networks).
    \item \textbf{Crime script analysis}: analysing investigative and judicial cases, news from the press.
    \item \textbf{Critical quantitative analysis of secondary sources}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Crime Script of the Online Trade}

The online trade can be seen as a process rather than a single event, involving stages in which resources and locations are required and decisions are made. The focus is mainly on the \textbf{supply side}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Preparation}: identification of the criminal opportunities to commit the online trade in falsified medicinal products.
    \item \textbf{Pre-activity}: logistical or transactional instrumental actions prior to the activity.
    \item \textbf{Activity}: concrete actions necessary to carry out the trade, which can take the form of various offences of non-compliance.
    \item \textbf{Post-activity}: logistical or transactional activities necessary to leave the scene.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Role of the Internet in Distribution}

There are significant geographical differences in distribution channels:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Developing countries}: legal distribution chain.
    \item \textbf{Developed countries}: illegal distribution chain (illegal websites, online marketplaces, social media websites, etc.).
\end{itemize}

An estimated \textbf{94\%} of online pharmacies (around 34,000) operate illegally.

\subsection{Indicators of an Illegal Online Pharmacy}

\begin{itemize}
    \item No request of a prescription for prescription-only medicines
    \item Low prices, promotions, and offer of trial packs
    \item Use of different languages, grammar mistakes
    \item Absence of a physical address
    \item Promotion of medicinal products on the home page
    \item Great attention to providing anonymity
    \item Use of ``testimonials''
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Distribution Channels}

Falsified medicines are distributed through multiple channels: social media platforms (Facebook, Twitter, Instagram), online marketplaces (e.g.\ Alibaba), and the dark web (e.g.\ the former Silk Road marketplace).

\subsection{EU Common Logo for Online Sale of Medicines}

As of 1 July 2015, all legally operating online pharmacies/retailers in the EU must display a common logo. The logo must be clearly displayed on every page of the website and links to the website of the national competent authority and to the national list of legally operating online pharmacies/retailers.

However, the system is \textbf{vulnerable to fraudsters}. A vulnerability assessment demonstrated that the EU common logo and the verification scheme can be counterfeited in a few minutes, with basic computer skills, spending only the money necessary to purchase a fraudulent domain. The steps are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Copy the logo from a legitimate website.
    \item Copy the institutional webpage of the national authority.
    \item Falsify the institutional webpage to list the illegal pharmacy.
    \item Purchase a fraudulent domain with a similar URL.
\end{enumerate}

In addition, the verification scheme has not been well implemented in some cases: the logo is not displayed at all, or the link does not work or points to pages other than the national register.

\chapter{Cybercrime Typologies: Cyber-Dependent Crimes}

\section{Categories of Cybercrime}

The term \textbf{cybercrime} covers a wide range of deviant and criminal behaviours that are constantly changing and evolving. It is unlikely that there is an exhaustive list or universally accepted definition of cybercrime. More common definitions refer to categorisations of cybercrime (Phillips et al., 2022), and these categories are not rigid --- there is the possibility of partial overlap. Cybercrime is often defined using \textbf{dichotomies and trichotomies}.

\subsection{The Brenner--Wall Trichotomy}

Brenner (2007) proposed a dichotomy later extended by Wall (2010) into a trichotomy:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cyber-dependent crime}: crimes that arose with technology and cannot exist outside the digital world.
    \item \textbf{Cyber-enabled crime}: the process of digitalisation has facilitated the commission of these crimes.
    \item \textbf{Cyber-assisted crime}: traditional crimes in which the use of the computer is not essential but rather incidental.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Gordon--Ford and Sarre--Lau--Chang Trichotomy}

Gordon and Ford (2006) distinguished:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Type I}: technical cybercrimes.
    \item \textbf{Type II}: cybercrimes that involve human contact.
\end{itemize}

Sarre, Lau, and Chang (2018) added a third category:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Type III}: crimes perpetuated by AI, robots, bots, and self-learning technology.
\end{itemize}


\section{Cyber-Dependent Crimes}

\begin{definition}[Cyber-dependent crime]
High-tech crimes that can only be committed using computers, computer networks, or other forms of ICT (Wall, 2005; Europol, 2017). These are crimes that \textbf{can only be committed in cyberspace} and would not exist without the Internet or technology. They represent a new form of crime that has emerged with the digital society --- also referred to as \textit{true cyber crime} or \textit{real cyber crime}.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Subcategories by Mode of Execution}

Based on how they are carried out, cyber-dependent crimes can be divided into \textbf{two subcategories} (McGuire \& Dowling, 2013):

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Illicit intrusions into computer networks}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Hacking
        \item Unauthorised access to computer networks, computers, and mobile devices exploiting security gaps and vulnerabilities in order to collect personal data and useful information, or to deface websites and launch DoS/DDoS attacks
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Interruption or damage to the functioning of computers and networks}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Malware
        \item Spam
        \item DoS
        \item DDoS
        \item Botnets
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Subcategories by CIA Triad}

Brar and Kumar (2018) proposed a \textbf{threefold definition} based on the pillars of cybersecurity:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Attacks on confidentiality}: eavesdropping, traffic analysis, password attacks, snooping, keyloggers, social engineering.
    \item \textbf{Attacks on integrity}: salami attacks, data diddling, cross-site scripting (XSS), SQL injection, session hijacking.
    \item \textbf{Attacks on availability}: DoS, DDoS.
\end{itemize}


\section{Case Studies}

\subsection{Ransomware Attacks on Hospitals}

On 3 August 2023, a ransomware attack hit a California-based healthcare system operating 16 hospitals and more than 165 clinics and centres across four US states. As a result, several services were shut down, facilities closed, patients and ambulances diverted, and digital records were unavailable. Cyberattacks on hospitals are on the rise worldwide (Argaw, Bempong, Eshaya-Chauvin et al., 2019).

\subsection{Ransomware Attacks on Law Enforcement}

In August 2023, a data breach at a company that prints ID cards and lanyards compromised data from customers including Greater Manchester Police and the London Metropolitan Police. The exposed data included names, ranks, photos, and serial numbers. Europol conducted an operation that led to the arrest of a member of the Ragnar Locker group and the seizure of its infrastructure.

\subsection{DDoS Attacks}

DDoS attacks are one of the biggest cyber threats and have evolved to avoid detection and increase damage (Bergamini de Neira, Kantarci, Nogueira, 2023). In October 2023, a record-breaking DDoS attack was reported against Google, Cloudflare, and AWS, peaking at over 398 million requests per second (RPS). Amazon (June 2020) and Google (2022) had previously reported stopping the then-largest DDoS attacks. The scale has grown from 26 to 398 million requests per second.

\subsection{Ashley Madison}

In July 2015, a hacker collective called ``The Impact Team'' infiltrated Ashley Madison --- a dating website for those seeking extramarital affairs --- stealing over 60 gigabytes of sensitive data (user profiles, financial transactions, sexual orientations, internal communications). Personal information of approximately 32 million users was exposed, leading to personal crises, suicides, and allegations of fraud and deceptive business practices by the platform.

\subsection{The Role of Artificial Intelligence}

AI introduces new dimensions to cyber-dependent crime:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acoustic side-channel attacks}: researchers developed deep-learning systems capable of hacking passwords using key acoustics with over 90\% accuracy (Harrison, Toreini, Mehrnezhad).
    \item \textbf{AI-enhanced social engineering}: chatbots such as ChatGPT can be exploited to create convincing phishing emails by correcting spelling and grammatical errors that would otherwise alert human readers. ``ChatGPT may therefore provide criminals with new opportunities, particularly for crimes involving social engineering'' (Europol, 2023).
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Cybercrime Typologies: Cyber-enabled Crimes}
% ========================================

\section{Introduction and Definition}

The digital society has given a \textbf{strong boost to a number of traditional crimes} that have been enhanced by the use of computers, computer networks, or other forms of information and communication technology (ICT). In contrast to cyber-dependent crime, \textbf{cyber-enabled crime} (McGuire \& Dowling, 2013) \textbf{can also be committed without the use of the digital world}: it is not born with technology, but technology transforms its scale, reach, and ease of commission.

A key distinction: cyber-enabled crime \textit{has always existed}, but it is now more \textbf{ubiquitous}, \textbf{faster}, and more \textbf{global}. The internet does not create new criminal motivations, but it dramatically expands opportunities and reduces barriers.

\begin{definition}[Cyber-enabled crime]
Traditional crimes for which the Internet has opened up entirely new possibilities and opportunities; crimes that are facilitated or have been made easier (i.e., enabled) by cyber technology (Phillips et al., 2022). These are crimes where the computer plays a role but that could still be committed without the involvement of the computer. Cyber-enabled crimes range from white-collar crime to drug trafficking, to online harassment, terrorism, and beyond.

Also referred to as:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hybrid crimes} (Wall, 2005)
    \item \textbf{Computer-related crimes} (Broadhead et al., 2018)
    \item \textbf{People crimes} (Black et al., 2019)
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
The boundary between cyber-dependent and cyber-enabled crime is not always clear-cut. The categories are analytically useful but descriptively fluid: the same conduct can shift categories depending on the specific modus operandi employed.
\end{remark}


\section{Subcategories of Cyber-enabled Crime}

Cyber-enabled crimes are organised into two broad clusters based on the nature of the harm caused:

\subsection{Cyber-enabled Crimes Related to Economy}

This cluster includes offences in which the primary motive is financial gain and the digital environment provides the infrastructure, anonymity, and scale needed to commit them:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Fraud (e-commerce fraud, mass-marketing frauds, phishing scams, pharming scams, ``online romance'' frauds)
    \item Intellectual property crimes
    \item Sale of illegal items on online marketplaces (including the deep or dark web)
    \item Cyber-laundering
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cyber-enabled Crimes Related to Interpersonal Violence}

This cluster encompasses offences targeting individuals, often with psychological, reputational, or sexual harm as the primary outcome:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cyber-bullying
    \item Cyber-mobbing
    \item Cyber-stalking
    \item Cyberharassment
    \item Sextortion
    \item Online grooming
    \item Trolling
    \item Griefing
    \item Assisted cyber suicide
    \item Image-based abuse (IBA)
    \item Production and dissemination of child pornography
\end{itemize}


\section{Cyber-enabled Crimes Related to Economy}

\subsection{The Industrialisation of Fraud}

The world of fraud is one of the areas that has benefited most from the introduction of the internet. The digital society has \textbf{industrialised fraud}: it lowered the barriers to entry, allowed offenders to reach exponentially more victims simultaneously, and reduced the risk of detection and punishment. Digital society is therefore particularly criminogenic in terms of fraud --- so much so that one could coin the adjective ``\textbf{fraudogenic}'' (Button \& Cross, 2017).

Characteristics that make online fraud structurally different from traditional fraud:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scale}: a single offender can target thousands of victims simultaneously.
    \item \textbf{Anonymity}: digital identities are easy to fabricate and difficult to trace.
    \item \textbf{Globalisation}: victims and perpetrators can be in entirely different jurisdictions, complicating prosecution.
    \item \textbf{Low detection risk}: compared to in-person fraud, the likelihood of identification and punishment is significantly reduced.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Fraud Typologies}

McGuire and Dowling (2013) identify the following typologies of online fraud:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Online frauds and e-commerce frauds}: broad category covering any deceptive scheme conducted via the internet or digital commerce platforms. Includes misrepresentation of goods, non-delivery of purchased items, and payment fraud.

    \item \textbf{Fraudulent sales}: carried out via online auction or retail sites, or through entirely bogus websites created specifically to defraud buyers. The seller collects payment but delivers counterfeit, inferior, or no goods at all.

    \item \textbf{Online mass marketing scams}: individuals are tricked into paying money upfront --- for example, to help someone, to participate in a business opportunity, or to claim a supposed prize --- with the promise that they will receive a larger sum of money in return at a later date. Classic examples include advance-fee fraud (``419 scams'') and lottery scams.

    \item \textbf{Phishing scams}: a user tricks the victim into providing personal information, financial details, or access codes by posing as a reputable entity or person in an email or other form of communication. The communication typically mimics the branding and language of legitimate institutions (banks, government agencies, e-commerce platforms).

    \item \textbf{Pharming scams}: a user is directed to a fake website --- sometimes via phishing emails, sometimes through DNS poisoning --- to enter their personal information. Unlike phishing, pharming can redirect users even when they type the correct URL into their browser.

    \item \textbf{Online romance scams}: the victim is contacted via social networks or dating websites and subjected to a prolonged online `relationship', at the end of which they are tricked into handing over personal details or large sums of money. Perpetrators typically fabricate elaborate identities (military personnel stationed abroad, wealthy professionals) and cultivate trust over weeks or months.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Beyond Fraud: Other Economic Cyber-enabled Crimes}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intellectual property crimes}: product piracy, counterfeiting, forgery, and the sale of counterfeit goods enabled by global online marketplaces. Digital distribution has dramatically lowered the cost of distributing pirated software, films, music, and physical goods.

    \item \textbf{Sale of illegal items on online marketplaces}: the internet, and particularly the deep and dark web, hosts marketplaces where users can find and purchase weapons, drugs, human organs, stolen data, hacking tools, and a variety of illegal services. The dark web provides layers of anonymity through tools such as Tor that make both buyers and sellers difficult to identify.

    \item \textbf{Cyber laundering}: criminals can clean the proceeds of illegal activities using cyber laundering systems. Some or all stages of the classical money laundering process (placement, layering, integration) may take place in cyberspace --- through cryptocurrency transactions, online gambling platforms, or fictitious e-commerce sales --- in order to erase the criminal origin of assets.
\end{itemize}


\section{Cyber-enabled Crimes Related to Interpersonal Violence}

\subsection{Malicious and Offensive Communications}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cyber-bullying}: intimidation and bullying of young people (less often children) through ICTs, including the creation of special websites with material about the person being persecuted, or by posting offensive reviews and comments. Cyber-bullying can be carried out by individuals or by coordinated groups and often extends into the physical world, affecting the victim's school and social life.

    \item \textbf{Cyber-mobbing}: psychological violence in the form of bullying that uses electronic communication to insult, threaten, or humiliate a victim. Unlike cyber-bullying (which tends to target peers in school settings), cyber-mobbing is typically carried out by a group of people who collectively bully one of their group members or any user, often in workplace or community contexts.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Online Violence Against Other Individuals}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cyber-stalking}: the systematic pursuit of a person, a group of people, or an organisation via the internet with the aim of intimidating them, blackmailing them, and making demands. It involves repeated, targeted conduct that causes fear or distress in the victim. Digital tools allow stalkers to monitor victims' movements, contacts, and routines at a level of detail previously impossible.

    \item \textbf{Cyber-harassment}: a repeated psychological cyberattack through persistent insults, harassment, demands, and virtual aggression. It typically manifests as a continuous flow of numerous messages, intrusive calls around the clock, and conversations of a humiliating and insulting nature. The persistence and visibility of online harassment can amplify harm well beyond what equivalent in-person conduct would cause.

    \item \textbf{Image-based abuse (IBA)}: a continuum of different forms of sexual violence that includes:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Non-consensual intimate image (NCII) distribution
        \item Revenge porn
        \item Deepfakes or fake pornography (AI-generated sexual imagery using real individuals' likenesses)
        \item Sextortion (the use or threatened use of intimate images to coerce victims into further sexual acts or payments)
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Online Sexual Offences Against Children}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Online grooming}: a form of sexual abuse of children and commercial sexual exploitation in which adult users employ digital platforms to establish trust with underage victims, with the ultimate intention of luring them into sexual contact or exploitation. Grooming typically unfolds over time, with the offender progressively desensitising the victim to sexual content.

    \item \textbf{Production and distribution of child pornography}: online platforms, including the dark web, are used to produce, share, and trade child sexual abuse material (CSAM). The digital environment has enabled the formation of large, international networks of offenders who share material and coordinate offending.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Other Forms of Cyber-enabled Interpersonal Crime}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Trolling}: social provocation in which virtual conversation partners are deliberately drawn into controversy in order to stir up a scandal or inflict emotional distress in network communication. Trolling ranges from low-level harassment to coordinated campaigns targeting individuals.

    \item \textbf{Griefing}: the deliberate infliction of moral and material damage on participants in online computer games --- sabotaging gameplay, destroying items, or psychologically tormenting other players. While often considered a minor infraction, in some cases it shades into extortion or fraud.

    \item \textbf{Assisted cyber suicide}: a form of psychological abuse in which the victim is driven to suicide by psychological manipulation, sustained pressure, or encouragement on the internet. It sits at the intersection of cyber-harassment and criminal facilitation of suicide.
\end{itemize}


\section{Case Studies}

\subsection{E-commerce Fraud: Belgian VAT Scheme (March 2023)}

In March 2023, Operation ``Silk Road'' uncovered a large-scale VAT fraud scheme involving Chinese e-commerce exporters. The perpetrators evaded Belgian import taxes by falsely declaring that consumer goods (electronics, toys, accessories) were destined for other EU Member States, thereby qualifying for a VAT exemption. The scheme relied on a network of bogus companies registered in France, Germany, Hungary, Italy, Poland, and Spain, supported by false invoices, forged transport documents, and fictitious identities. The total amount of VAT fraud was estimated at \textbf{303 million euros}. The case illustrates how cross-border digital trade creates systematic vulnerabilities in tax enforcement regimes, and how international criminal networks can exploit regulatory gaps between Member States.

\subsection{Cyber Laundering: Estonian Cryptocurrency Fraud (November 2022)}

In November 2022, two Estonian nationals were arrested in Tallinn for their involvement in a multi-layered fraud and money laundering scheme centred on cryptocurrencies. The perpetrators operated on two fronts: they tricked victims into signing fraudulent equipment rental contracts, and separately induced victims to invest in a fictitious virtual currency bank. In total, victims paid more than \textbf{\$575 million} to the defendants' companies. The proceeds were then laundered through shell companies and used to purchase real estate and luxury cars in Estonia. The case demonstrates the intersection of classic investment fraud with cyber laundering, and the use of cryptocurrency's opacity to obscure illicit financial flows.

\subsection{Online Romance Fraud (March 2023, Italy)}

In March 2023, Italian police arrested eight individuals for serious fraud, money laundering, and counterfeiting, following an investigation into a romance scam network. Victims (primarily women) were contacted on Facebook by a fake profile named ``Larry Brooks'', a self-proclaimed US Army officer stationed in Syria. Over the period 2018--2021, the criminal group defrauded \textbf{32 victims} of approximately \textbf{\texteuro 400,000} in total. The operation relied on a well-established and adaptable modus operandi, with the script adjusted according to the victim's profile. The case exemplifies the psychological sophistication of modern romance fraud: victims, often elderly or isolated, transferred their savings --- including pension payments --- in the belief they were supporting a loved one.

\subsection{Sextortion: The Jordan DeMay Case (March 2022)}

In March 2022, Jordan DeMay, a 17-year-old American high school football player from Michigan, was contacted via an Instagram account (``dani.robertts'') that appeared to belong to a young woman. The account had in fact been hacked and sold to a 22-year-old Nigerian man, who used it to coerce young men into sending explicit images of themselves, then threatened to distribute the images unless money was paid. DeMay died by suicide within hours of being targeted. The suspects, Samuel and Samson Ogoshi, were subsequently extradited to the United States and charged in federal court. The case became a landmark in debates about the criminal liability for sextortion-induced suicide and the cross-jurisdictional challenges of prosecuting online sexual coercion.

\begin{keypoint}
The DeMay case exemplifies the link between IBA and assisted cyber suicide: sextortion does not merely constitute a financial or reputational harm, but in the most severe cases constitutes a precipitating cause of death. It also illustrates how the perpetration of sextortion does not require the offender to possess any intimate images in advance --- the scheme is engineered to create them.
\end{keypoint}

\subsection{Online Sexual Crimes Against Children: The Love Zone}

The Love Zone (TLZ) was one of the largest pedophile online communities in the world, with approximately \textbf{45,000 members}. The platform was used to disseminate pedophile practices and to sell, procure, and trade child sexual abuse material (CSAM). In 2014, Queensland Police identified the TLZ administrator, covertly assumed control of his account, and monitored the site for six months, exposing the forum's most active and prolific members in a classic law enforcement ``honeypot'' operation. The operation ultimately led to dozens of arrests across multiple jurisdictions; the most recent arrests linked to TLZ date to May and August 2023. One individual associated with the network was charged with 1,623 child abuse offences. The case highlights the tension between the investigative value of covertly operating illegal platforms (allowing broader identification of offenders) and the ethical and legal implications of temporarily permitting ongoing criminal activity.

\chapter{Cybercrime Typologies: Cyber-assisted Crimes}
% ========================================

\section{Definition}

Cyber-assisted crime is the third and least technology-dependent category in the Brenner--Wall trichotomy. These are traditional crimes in which the internet is used ``to assist with the organisation of a crime'' (Wall, 2005). The term \textit{cyber-assisted crime} was formally proposed by McGuire and Dowling (2013).

\begin{definition}[Cyber-assisted crime]
Traditional crimes in which the digital tool is \textbf{not critical}: the crime ``would still take place if the internet was removed'' (Wall, 2017). Technology is part of more complex offences or criminal activities, used to \textbf{assist pre-existing operations} or by moving part of the criminal business online (Wall, 2017; Di Nicola, 2022). The result is what Wall (2017) calls \textbf{cyber-assisted forms of organisation}.
\end{definition}

The key distinction from cyber-enabled crime is one of degree, not of kind: in cyber-assisted crime, digital tools intervene at specific phases of a criminal activity that is structurally independent of them. Remove the internet and the crime still happens; with the internet, it becomes more efficient, more scalable, and harder to disrupt.

\begin{remark}
The three categories --- cyber-dependent, cyber-enabled, cyber-assisted --- form a spectrum of digital integration, from crimes that cannot exist without technology, to crimes that merely use technology as an incidental organisational tool. Most real-world organised crime today operates somewhere along this spectrum rather than at a single fixed point.
\end{remark}


\section{Crime Scripts}

\subsection{The Concept}

To understand cyber-assisted crime, it is useful to adopt the analytical framework of \textbf{crime scripts} (Cornish, 1994). A crime script is a structured description of the sequential steps involved in committing a crime, identifying the actions taken, the resources required, and the decision points at which intervention is possible. Applied to cyber-assisted crime, crime scripts allow us to map exactly \textit{where} and \textit{how} digital tools intervene in an otherwise traditional criminal process.

Key features of the crime script approach:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Crime is treated as a process that can be ``unfolded into separate but related phases'' (Lavorgna, 2013).
    \item The influence of the digital society may be limited to \textbf{one or more phases} of the criminal activity, not necessarily the whole.
    \item The method was recommended by Cornish and Clarke (2002) specifically for investigating organised crime.
    \item The goal is to understand the role of the internet, the opportunities it creates, and the phases in which those opportunities occur --- providing law enforcement with \textbf{intervention points}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Example: Tag Writing (Cornish, 1994)}

The classic illustrative example of a crime script is graffiti tag writing. Each stage has a corresponding script action and a corresponding situational control measure that could prevent or disrupt it:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Scene/Function} & \textbf{Script Action} & \textbf{Situational Control} \\
\midrule
Preparation & Buy spray-can; find a good setting & Sales regulation; city paint-out program \\
Entry & Enter setting & Access control; entry/exit screening \\
Pre-condition & Loiter & Surveillance \\
Instrumental pre-condition & Select target & Remove target \\
Instrumental initiation & Approach target & Surveillance \\
Instrumental actualization & Reach target & Protective screens; legal target provided \\
Doing & Spray graffiti & Graffiti-resistant paint \\
Post-condition & Get away quietly & Moisture-activated alarm \\
Exit & Leave setting & Entry/exit screening \\
Doing (later) & ``Getting up'' & Rapid cleaning \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The value of the script is not the description of any individual act but the systematic identification of \textit{all} the stages at which prevention could interrupt the sequence. The same logic applies to complex organised crimes.

\subsection{Application: Wildlife Trafficking (Lavorgna, 2013)}

Wildlife trafficking is a paradigmatic cyber-assisted crime: a multi-stage smuggling operation encompassing numerous activities (Wyatt, 2022), in which the internet plays a \textbf{crucial role specifically in the advertising and selling stages} --- making it easier to find potential buyers or sellers (Lavorgna, 2020) --- while physical logistics remain offline.

The crime script of wildlife trafficking (Lavorgna, 2013: 78) unfolds across seven stages:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{Stage 0}] Preparatory activities antecedent to the commission of wildlife trafficking.
    \item[\textbf{Stage 1}] Poaching, harvesting, or breeding of the animal or plant.
    \item[\textbf{Stage 2}] Intermediate passage through local middlemen and the domestic market.
    \item[\textbf{Stage 3}] Passage through regional middlemen and international traders.
    \item[\textbf{Stage 4}] Intermediate passage through local middlemen and the domestic market (at destination).
    \item[\textbf{Stage 5}] Distribution of the animal, plant, or product.
    \item[\textbf{Stage 6}] Activities directly consequential or subsequent to the trafficking activity.
\end{enumerate}

Lavorgna's detailed mapping of internet use across these stages (2013: 79--81) shows that digital tools appear across all stages as \textbf{Crime}, \textbf{Lifestyle}, and \textbf{Network} script categories. Selected examples:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Preparation (Stage 0)}: Formation of criminal networks via email; use of dedicated forums to exchange information on potential buyers; use of Google Translate to post announcements in different languages; communication with other members of the network about relevant legislation.
    \item \textbf{Entry (Stage 0, 1, 4)}: Discussion with criminal peers to exchange information on potential new buyers; interaction with potential buyers via auction websites, general commercial websites, dedicated commercial websites, dedicated forums.
    \item \textbf{Pre-condition}: Maintenance of contacts with criminal network members (email, Skype); signalling via dedicated websites of recently discovered species that are rare and valuable; obtaining information on specific species (morphology, breeding); sharing information about the location of the animal/plant in its natural environment (dedicated blogs); agreement to encourage local middlemen who hesitate due to fear of prosecution; informing potential clients and middlemen of updated prices.
    \item \textbf{Instrumental pre-condition (Stages 3, 4)}: Agreement to exclude local middlemen who hesitate; search for new suppliers via emails and dedicated forums.
    \item \textbf{Instrumental actualization (Stages 3--5)}: Advertising via fairs and exhibitions (dedicated websites, forums); forwarding emails from suppliers who can fulfil orders; identifying the location of the animal via GPS and web mapping services (e.g.\ Google Maps); ordering/reserving specimens on dedicated commercial websites; arranging sales of the specimen to middlemen via international auction websites; discussing prices and meeting places in the physical world to close deals (email, domains); discussing via criminal network how to ship specimens in small packages with deceptive labels and then send them through regular mail; agreement to physically meet and exchange animals/products (private messaging on dedicated commercial websites, email); putting customers directly in contact with middlemen or suppliers; contacting customers to reassure them that, without documents, the price is significantly lower; discussing with potential customers the absence of required documentation; reassuring customers about the quality of the animal/plant/product on dedicated commercial websites and auction websites.
    \item \textbf{Doing (Stages 1, 4, and later)}: Auctioning/selling the animal/plant/product (dedicated commercial websites); selling on auction websites (e.g.\ eBay); agreement to send animals/plants/products via regular mail among middlemen; monitoring delivery via regular mail; payment via prepaid cards that can be recharged online.
    \item \textbf{Post-condition (Stages 4--6)}: Buying accessories and sharing videos and pictures in zoos or cases where wild animals are involved (dedicated forums, deep web); attempting to cover the criminal act by claiming that animals/plants/products have been imported in the past with CITES certificates but have been given to others (dedicated website).
\end{itemize}

This detailed mapping reveals that the internet penetrates every stage of the wildlife trafficking script, yet the crime itself --- the physical capture, transport, and delivery of animals --- remains independent of digital infrastructure. This is the hallmark of cyber-assisted crime.


\section{New Criminal Opportunities}

Modern technological tools have created \textbf{new criminal opportunities} for organised groups (Lavorgna, 2013, 2015a, 2015b). Understanding this dimension is essential for grasping the full impact of digital society on criminal activities, organised or otherwise.

Lavorgna identifies \textbf{five types of opportunities} available to criminal groups:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Opportunities for communication}: encrypted messaging platforms, dedicated forums, and anonymous communication tools allow criminal groups to coordinate at scale and across borders with greatly reduced risk of interception.

    \item \textbf{Management opportunities}: digital tools facilitate the logistics of criminal operations --- inventory management, financial transactions, coordination of supply chains --- making large and geographically dispersed operations manageable.

    \item \textbf{Organisational opportunities}: the internet enables new forms of criminal organisation, including loosely networked structures that are more resilient to law enforcement disruption than traditional hierarchical groups. Members may never meet physically.

    \item \textbf{Relational opportunities}: online platforms allow criminal groups to identify, vet, and recruit new members, build trust relationships with suppliers and buyers, and maintain ongoing relationships with co-offenders and clients across jurisdictions.

    \item \textbf{Promotional, marketing, persuasive, and fidelization opportunities}: criminal groups use the internet to advertise services and products to potential customers, build a reputation, manage reviews and feedback (as seen in dark web marketplaces), and retain clients through loyalty mechanisms analogous to legitimate e-commerce.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Cyber-assisted Criminal Activities}

Cyber-assisted crimes are divided into two broad clusters based on who commits them:

\subsection{Trafficking Activities of Collectivities}

These are multi-actor, multi-stage operations in which organised criminal groups use digital tools to assist pre-existing trafficking activities:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Counterfeit medicine trafficking}: the internet facilitates the sourcing, advertising, and distribution of falsified pharmaceutical products, as explored in Project FAKECARE.
    \item \textbf{Smuggling of migrants and asylum seekers}: as documented in the Surf and Sound project, social media platforms and messaging apps are used at every stage of the smuggling process --- recruitment, route planning, real-time navigation, and feedback.
    \item \textbf{Trafficking for the purpose of exploitation} (particularly sexual): digital recruitment tools, fake job advertisements, and loverboy methods are used to identify and groom victims before physical exploitation occurs.
    \item \textbf{Terrorism and radicalisation}: the internet is used as a recruitment and propaganda tool, for coordination of attacks, and for dissemination of extremist ideology --- while the physical acts of violence remain independent of digital infrastructure.
    \item \textbf{Sale of counterfeit products or psychotropic substances}: online marketplaces (surface web, deep web, dark web) facilitate supply, demand, and payment for a wide range of illicit goods.
    \item \textbf{Illegal animal trafficking}: as illustrated by the wildlife crime script above.
    \item \textbf{Tobacco trade}: illicit tobacco trafficking uses online platforms for sourcing, ordering, and coordinating distribution networks.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Criminal Activities of Individuals}

Beyond organised crime, individual criminals also exploit digital tools in an assistive capacity:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Using the internet to learn how to commit or hide a crime}: online tutorials, forums, and dark web resources provide information on criminal techniques, from document forgery to evading forensic detection.
    \item \textbf{Using social media accounts to gather useful information about prospective victims}: OSINT (open-source intelligence) techniques applied to social media allow individual criminals to profile targets, identify vulnerabilities, and plan offences with a level of detail previously available only to professional investigators.
    \item \textbf{Crime-as-a-service}: the digital market allows individuals to outsource specific criminal tasks --- hacking, money mule recruitment, document forgery, phishing kit deployment --- without possessing the skills themselves, lowering the barrier to entry for complex criminal activity.
\end{itemize}


\section{Case Studies}

\subsection{Encrypted Criminal Communications: EncroChat (2020)}

EncroChat was an encrypted communication tool used on a large scale by organised criminal groups for criminal purposes. With an estimated \textbf{60,000 users}, the platform provided hardware-modified phones running a hardened operating system with features specifically designed to frustrate law enforcement: remote wipe capability, no GPS logging, and end-to-end encrypted messaging. In 2020, a joint investigation by French and Dutch authorities infiltrated the EncroChat network and was able to intercept, share, and analyse millions of communications in real time. The operation led to \textbf{over 6,500 arrests} across multiple countries and the seizure of close to \textbf{EUR 900 million}. Similar operations subsequently dismantled two further encrypted criminal communication networks: \textbf{Exclu} and \textbf{Sky ECC}.

The EncroChat case exemplifies the ``opportunities for communication'' that digital tools provide to organised crime. It also illustrates the law enforcement response: rather than blocking the communications, authorities chose to covertly monitor them, harvesting intelligence on criminal networks at an unprecedented scale. This raises significant questions about admissibility of evidence, bulk surveillance, and the rule of law that continue to be litigated in courts across Europe.

\subsection{Human Trafficking and Technology}

Technology plays an increasingly important role in trafficking in human beings (THB), particularly in the two key phases of the trafficking process: \textbf{recruitment} and \textbf{exploitation} of victims.

\paragraph{Recruitment phase.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fake job advertisements}: attractive work conditions (high pay, foreign travel, hospitality work) are advertised on legitimate job platforms and social networks, luring victims into exploitative situations before the deception becomes apparent.
    \item \textbf{Loverboy method}: the trafficker cultivates a fake romantic relationship with the victim online, building emotional dependence before introducing conditions of exploitation. This method is particularly effective against young and socially isolated individuals.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Exploitation phase.}
Once recruited, digital tools are used to control victims (e.g.\ confiscating phones, monitoring communications), advertise victims' services online, collect payments, and launder proceeds.

The GRETA report (\textit{Online and technology-facilitated trafficking in human beings}) documents the systematic integration of digital tools into THB operations and the corresponding need for law enforcement responses adapted to the digital environment. A 2023 hackathon specifically targeted the problem of human traffickers luring victims online, bringing together technologists and law enforcement to develop detection tools.

\subsection{Illicit Online Markets}

Digital technologies are used to facilitate the sale of a wide range of illegal and contraband goods. Key categories include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Counterfeit products}: online platforms (including social media and dark web marketplaces) are used to advertise, sell, and ship counterfeit goods. An international operation by Europol shut down 12,526 websites offering counterfeit goods and pirated content, seizing 127,365 counterfeit products worth EUR 3.8 million.
    \item \textbf{Illegal dark web markets}: dedicated marketplaces on the dark web provide a full-service environment for buying and selling drugs, weapons, documents, and other contraband. Law enforcement on three continents arrested 288 dark web vendors in a major marketplace seizure, seizing EUR 50.8 million in cash and virtual currencies, 850 kg of drugs, and 117 firearms.
    \item \textbf{Wildlife and endangered species}: as analysed through the crime script framework above.
    \item \textbf{Cultural heritage artifacts}: the internet facilitates the illicit trade in looted or stolen cultural heritage objects. An international art trafficking operation led to 60 arrests and the recovery of over 11,000 objects.
    \item \textbf{Pharmaceuticals}: online markets, including end-to-end encrypted messaging platforms such as Telegram, are used to distribute both controlled and uncontrolled substances. A documented case involved an underground abortion pill market growing on Telegram as US states restricted access to abortion care, illustrating how legal and political context shapes the demand side of illicit online markets.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keypoint}
Across all three case studies, a common pattern emerges: the criminal activity itself (trafficking, violence, physical delivery of goods) is not digital, but the internet provides the infrastructure for communication, recruitment, advertising, payment, and logistics. This is the operational core of cyber-assisted crime. Law enforcement responses must therefore target not only the physical crime but also the digital layers that enable it --- and doing so raises complex questions about privacy, jurisdiction, and the proportionality of surveillance.
\end{keypoint}

\lecturepart{Lecture A --- AI and ML and Cybercrime}

\section{Human--Machine Interaction and Crime}

The literature on digital sociology emphasises the \textbf{relationship between the human being and the machine in the construction of the new digital society}, in a socio-technical vision. In a world where robots support or replace humans, and where artificial intelligence is gaining ground, a fundamental criminological question arises: are there, or will there be, increasingly \textbf{hybrid criminals} --- the result of close interaction between humans and computers?

King et al.\ (2020: 91) define phenomena of ``\textbf{crime perpetrated by artificial intelligence}'' in which the perpetrator is very difficult to circumscribe, and in which some questions arise spontaneously: \textit{Who commits the crime? A human agent? An artificial agent? Both of them?} These questions challenge the anthropocentric assumptions at the core of traditional criminology and criminal law.

\section{Artificial Intelligence: The Concept}

\begin{definition}[Artificial intelligence]
Artificial intelligence deals with \textbf{artificial agents capable of carrying out tasks that usually require intelligence and human intervention to be conducted}. These artificial agents are ``sufficiently informed'', ``intelligent'', ``autonomous and capable of carrying out morally relevant actions independently of the humans who created them'' (Floridi and Sanders, 2004: 351).
\end{definition}

The key criminological implication of this definition is the concept of \textbf{moral autonomy}: an AI system that can take actions independently of its creators creates a category of harmful conduct that sits outside the traditional offender--victim dyad. This forces criminology to reckon with questions of agency, responsibility, and attribution that existing frameworks were not designed to handle.

\section{AI and Criminology: Three Categories of Challenge}

AI introduces new challenges for crime and criminology along three axes:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Crime with artificial intelligence}
    \item \textbf{Crime against artificial intelligence}
    \item \textbf{Crime through artificial intelligence}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Crime with Artificial Intelligence}

AI can be a \textbf{powerful tool for criminal purposes}. As Brundage et al.\ (2018: 19--22) argue, AI in criminal hands is capable of:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Expanding existing threats}: allowing criminals to scale attacks that were previously limited by human labour (e.g.\ automated phishing at scale).
    \item \textbf{Introducing entirely new threats}: creating categories of harm that were not previously possible.
    \item \textbf{Completely altering the characteristics of existing threats}: changing the speed, accuracy, cost, or detectability of a known crime.
\end{itemize}

The paradigmatic example is the \textbf{deepfake}: AI-generated audio-visual content that realistically impersonates a real person. Deepfakes can be used for disinformation (fabricating statements by public figures), financial fraud (voice cloning to impersonate executives), and image-based sexual abuse (generating non-consensual intimate imagery using a real person's likeness). As noted in Part IV with the Crosetto case (2025), deepfake technology has already been operationally deployed in large-scale fraud.

\subsection{Crime against Artificial Intelligence}

Here we are dealing with \textbf{attacks that exploit the vulnerabilities of an AI system to manipulate or ``hypnotise'' the system itself}. The AI system is the victim of the attack, but the ultimate objective is typically to weaponise the system against third parties.

Key attack vectors include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training data poisoning}: the attacker corrupts the data used to train an AI model, causing the model to learn systematically distorted patterns. If the poisoned model is then deployed in a criminal justice, medical, or financial context, its decisions will be systematically biased in ways that benefit the attacker.
    \item \textbf{Adversarial inputs}: carefully crafted inputs that cause an AI system to misclassify or misbehave at inference time --- for example, fooling an image classifier or a facial recognition system.
    \item \textbf{Model reprogramming}: manipulating an AI system to commit criminal acts, behaving as an autonomous criminal agent.
\end{itemize}

The paradigmatic example is \textbf{Microsoft's Tay chatbot} (2016): a conversational AI deployed on Twitter that was manipulated within 24 hours through coordinated user input, causing it to generate racist and offensive content at scale. This is a case where coordinated social engineering constituted a form of ``crime against AI'' that turned the platform's own technology into a vector of harm --- with no clear individual perpetrator.

\subsection{Crime through Artificial Intelligence}

In this category AI becomes an \textbf{intermediary agent}: a non-human actor that stands between the human criminal and the criminal act, creating a layer of separation that complicates attribution and detection.

The clearest example is a \textbf{bot purchasing drugs on the dark web} instead of a natural person. The human criminal programmes the bot with intent and provides resources, but the actual criminal transaction is carried out autonomously by the AI. In this model, artificial intelligence acts as a \textbf{criminal shield} --- a screen between the offender and the offence that exploits the inability of legal systems to assign criminal liability to non-human agents.

\section{AI and Crime Areas}

AI-facilitated crime is not evenly distributed across crime types. The principal affected areas are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Identity theft and fraud, impersonation, and online fraud generally}: AI enables the creation of synthetic identities, voice and face cloning for impersonation, and automated phishing campaigns personalised at scale.
    \item \textbf{Financial crime}: algorithmic manipulation of markets, AI-driven money laundering through complex transaction patterns, and automated detection evasion.
    \item \textbf{Drug trafficking and trading}: AI bots as purchasing agents, algorithmic pricing and inventory management on dark web markets.
    \item \textbf{Harassment}: AI-generated hate speech, automated pile-ons, and the use of AI to identify and profile harassment targets.
    \item \textbf{Sex crimes}: deepfake pornography, AI-assisted grooming (chatbots maintaining grooming conversations at scale), and automated generation and distribution of child sexual abuse material.
\end{itemize}

\section{AI-enabled Future Crimes: Risk Matrix}

Caldwell, Andrews, Tanay et al.\ (\textit{Crime Science}, 9:14, 2020) systematically assessed AI-enabled future crimes along two dimensions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Harm or profit potential} (low to high)
    \item \textbf{Difficulty of defeating} (easier to harder)
\end{itemize}

The resulting risk matrix classifies AI-enabled crimes as follows:

\paragraph{High risk (high harm, hard to defeat):}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Audio/video impersonation (deepfakes)
    \item Tailored phishing (AI-personalised social engineering)
    \item AI-authored fake news
    \item Disrupting AI-controlled systems
    \item Large-scale blackmail
    \item Market bombing
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Medium risk:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Learning-based cyber attacks
    \item Tricking face recognition systems
    \item Online eviction
    \item Data poisoning
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Low risk (low harm or easier to defeat):}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bias exploitation
    \item AI-authored fake reviews
    \item Burglar bots
    \item AI-assisted stalking
    \item Forgery
    \item Evading AI detection
\end{itemize}

The matrix is useful for prioritising defensive investment: high-risk threats that are also difficult to defeat require urgent attention, while lower-risk or more easily defeated threats can be addressed with proportionally lower resources.

\section{Cyborg-Crime}

Working on botnets --- networks of infected computers controlled by a single operator, increasingly used to commit cybercrime --- van der Wagen and Pieters (2015) coined the concept of \textbf{cyborg-crime}: online crime perpetrated by a \textbf{cyborg actor}, and proposed the use of \textbf{Actor-Network Theory} (ANT) as a framework for interpreting forms of delinquency committed by actors who are \textit{neither completely human, nor completely driven by a machine}.

Their core argument: it is not possible to understand the nature of crimes carried out through botnets if we continue to use an \textbf{anthropocentric criminology} that sees the human being as the main force behind criminal behaviour. ``Rather than considering a botnet as a technological tool, an individual (opportunistic) crime or a resource on the criminal market, we treat a botnet as a \textbf{hybrid crime network}, a crime that derives from mutual cooperation and human/technology interaction'' (ibid.: 579).

The cyborg-crime concept bridges the previous three categories (with/against/through AI) and points towards a broader theoretical reconceptualisation of criminal agency in the digital age. It challenges criminology to develop frameworks capable of assigning responsibility, assessing harm, and designing interventions in contexts where the boundary between human and machine agency is systematically blurred.


\lecturepart{Lecture B --- Measuring Cybercrime: Data Sources and Challenges}

\section{Overview: Crime Data Sources}

Traditional crimes can be measured through three main data sources:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Official crime statistics}: quantitative data produced by the criminal justice system.
    \item \textbf{Victimisation surveys}: data collected directly from the population about their experience of crime.
    \item \textbf{Self-report surveys}: data collected from individuals about their own criminal or deviant behaviour.
\end{enumerate}

Cybercrime, however, is extremely difficult to measure and to reconcile across these sources. Each instrument faces specific challenges when applied to digital crime, compounding the already severe \textbf{dark figure of crime} problem.

\section{Official Crime Statistics}

\subsection{Definition and History}

\begin{definition}[Official crime statistics]
Quantitative data on crime recorded by state agencies, primarily police, prosecutors, and courts. Originated in France in the early 19th century, initially focused on judicial and penitentiary statistics and later extended to police-recorded data.
\end{definition}

Main administrative sources at the supranational level:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Interpol}: collected comparative data until 2006.
    \item \textbf{UN Crime Trends Survey (CTS)}: collects standardised crime statistics from member states.
    \item \textbf{European Sourcebook of Crime and Criminal Justice Statistics}: compiles cross-national data from European countries.
    \item \textbf{Eurostat database}: produces harmonised EU-level crime statistics.
\end{itemize}

Key structural problems:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The \textbf{dark figure of crime}: a large proportion of crimes are never reported to police, never recorded, or lost at subsequent stages of the criminal justice process. Official statistics therefore systematically undercount actual crime.
    \item \textbf{No detailed information on the criminal event, the victim, or the offender}: administrative records capture only what is legally and procedurally relevant, stripping out the contextual information needed for criminological analysis.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Official Statistics and Cybercrime}

Official statistics on cybercrime are \textbf{extremely limited}, for several structural reasons:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cybercrimes --- especially cyber-enabled ones --- are in most cases \textbf{registered as traditional crimes}. A phishing fraud is recorded as fraud; a sextortion case may be recorded as blackmail. The digital dimension is lost.
    \item There is a \textbf{lack of uniform registration} of cybercrime in the police registration systems of most Western countries: no agreed taxonomy, no mandatory cybercrime flag, no standardised coding.
    \item Information concerning the digital nature of a crime may be present in \textbf{unstructured qualitative text fields} in police or court registration systems --- but extracting it is a labour-intensive task not amenable to standard statistical processing.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Notable exceptions:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3)}: a dedicated reporting portal for internet crime in the United States, producing annual reports on cybercrime complaints and losses.
    \item \textbf{Uniform Crime Report Survey of Canada}: includes specific cybercrime categories.
    \item \textbf{European Sourcebook of Crime and Criminal Justice Statistics}: publishes cross-national records of cyber-enabled fraud and, for some countries, cyber-dependent crime.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Methodological response --- machine learning:}
To overcome the problem of cybercrime information buried in unstructured text fields, two research approaches have been developed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sessink (University of Twente): ``\textit{Using Machine Learning to Detect ICT in Criminal Court Cases}'' --- applying NLP classifiers to court documents to identify cases with an ICT dimension.
    \item Van der Laan and Tollenaar: ``\textit{Text Mining for Cybercrime in Registrations of the Dutch Police}'' --- using text mining to flag cybercrime-relevant entries in police records that would otherwise be invisible to quantitative analysis.
\end{itemize}

\section{Victimisation Surveys}

\subsection{Definition}

\begin{definition}[Victimisation survey]
A technique that involves asking a representative sample of the population: (1) whether and of what crimes they have been victims in a given period of time prior to the interview; and (2) if so, further details about the incident (where it occurred, at what time, and how).
\end{definition}

The key advantage over official statistics is that victimisation surveys capture crimes that were never reported to police, directly addressing the dark figure of crime.

\subsection{The US National Crime Victimization Survey (NCVS)}

The NCVS is the foundational instrument of modern victimisation research. Key milestones:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First pilot studies in the 1960s}: conducted on 10,000 people, respondents reported \textbf{double the crime rate recorded in official statistics} --- a landmark finding that demonstrated the scale of the dark figure and justified the survey approach.
    \item \textbf{Since 1972}: the NCVS has been continuously investigating the frequency, characteristics, and consequences of victimisation in the United States.
    \item \textbf{Coverage}: crimes against the person (rape and sexual assault, robbery, assault, bag snatching/pickpocketing) and against property (theft, burglary, motor vehicle theft).
\end{itemize}

The NCVS allows researchers to:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Estimate the total number of crimes} committed against the population as a whole --- consistently showing a far higher figure than official statistics (the gap between the NCVS and the Uniform Crime Report (UCR) is visually striking, with NCVS figures for violent crime running at several times the UCR count for the same years).
    \item \textbf{Investigate differential victimisation}: analyse the levels of victimisation to which different segments of the population are exposed, based on victim characteristics (age, gender, ethnicity, marital status, income, education). Example: males, Black individuals, and young people up to age 24 are more at risk of violent crime than women, white individuals, and those over 24.
    \item \textbf{Provide details about the perpetrator and the events}: capturing information on offender demographics, victim--offender relationship, and circumstances of the offence that official statistics do not record.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Impact of the NCVS:}
The success of the NCVS prompted the development of similar surveys at both international and national levels:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{International}: the \textbf{International Crime Victims Survey (ICVS)}.
    \item \textbf{National}: Netherlands (Victims of Crime, 1974), United Kingdom (British Crime Survey, 1982), Italy (1997).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Victimisation Surveys and Cybercrime}

The application of victimisation surveys to cybercrime is still developing. A systematic review by Reep-van den Bergh and Junger (\textit{Crime Science}, 7:5, 2018) --- ``\textit{Victims of cybercrime in Europe: a review of victim surveys}'' --- provides the most comprehensive picture to date:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Objective}: to map cyber-victimisation patterns in Europe and provide estimates of annual prevalence rates (number of victims per population) for six cybercrimes.
    \item \textbf{Methodology}: definition of survey inclusion criteria; database and internet searches; grey literature; contacts with national statistical offices.
    \item \textbf{Results}: nine surveys identified across European countries; highly heterogeneous annual prevalence rates depending on the crime type:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item 1--3\% for online shopping scams.
        \item 3\% of the population is a victim of cyberbullying.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

The wide variation in estimates across surveys --- arising from differences in operationalisation, reference periods, sampling frames, and question wording --- underlines how immature the field of cybercrime measurement still is. Comparability across surveys remains a major methodological challenge.

\section{Self-Report Surveys}

\subsection{Definition and Purpose}

Self-report surveys are instruments in which respondents are asked about instances in which they have been \textbf{actively involved in crime or deviant behaviour} --- i.e., as offenders rather than victims. They are particularly important for:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Measuring \textbf{juvenile delinquency}, where official statistics are especially unreliable due to selective enforcement and diversion.
    \item Developing and testing \textbf{theories of criminal and deviant behaviour}, since they provide data on the prevalence and frequency of offending in the general population rather than only among those caught by the system.
\end{itemize}

The key international self-report instrument is the \textbf{International Self-Report Delinquency Study (ISRD)}.

\begin{keypoint}
A recurring observation across all three measurement instruments: the dark figure of cybercrime is almost certainly larger than for traditional crime. Victims do not recognise they have been victimised; reporting rates are extremely low; official categories do not map onto cybercrime; and offenders who commit cybercrime often do not perceive their behaviour as criminal. Measuring the true extent of cybercrime remains one of the most significant methodological challenges in contemporary criminology.
\end{keypoint}

\section{Introduction and Context}

\subsection{Scope of Fraud and Computer Misuse}

Fraud and Computer Misuse (F\&CM) became part of yearly crime estimates produced by the UK Office for National Statistics (ONS) only in 2015, on the basis of the Crime Survey for England and Wales (CSEW). Recent estimates (year ending March 2020) reported approximately \textbf{4.6 million} incidents of fraud and \textbf{876{,}000} incidents of computer misuse, raising the overall crime estimate from about 5.7 million (without F\&CM) to \textbf{10.2 million} estimated crimes. Only a small fraction of these crimes is reported to police: about \textbf{8\%} of frauds and \textbf{2\%} of computer misuse incidents.

Despite low reporting rates, the absolute volume of recorded F\&CM has grown considerably, in part because of the rollout of \textbf{Action Fraud (AF)} as the National Reporting Centre. Following its national rollout in 2013, the volume of recorded frauds increased by over \textbf{160\%} between 2011 and 2013. Victim support services have been decentralised, with competitive bidding for providers funded through the Police and Crime Commissioners (PCCs).

\subsection{Attrition and the `Four Ps' Policing Strategy}

There is considerable \textit{attrition} within the criminal justice system: a small share of AF calls are recorded as crimes (`crimed'), fewer are reviewed for investigation, and fewer still reach courts. Most F\&CM victims therefore receive no meaningful CJS response. The UK \textit{Code of Practice for Victims of Crime} requires that vulnerable and repeat victims be identified and offered adequate support, but research on F\&CM repeat victims is limited and definitions of vulnerability are inconsistent across agencies.

\begin{sidebox}{The `Four Ps' approach}
F\&CM policing strategy in the UK has four strands, originally drawn from counter-terrorism policing:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pursue} --- investigate crimes to enable prosecution.
    \item \textbf{Prevent} --- prevent people from engaging in F\&CM (awareness of consequences, diversion of talented individuals into legal/ethical hacking).
    \item \textbf{Protect} --- increase protection for those at risk of (further) victimisation.
    \item \textbf{Prepare} --- improve resilience to reduce the impact of F\&CM in the future.
\end{itemize}
The paper's contribution falls within the \textbf{Protect} strand: targeting those already victimised to prevent re-victimisation.
\end{sidebox}

\section{Defining and Measuring Repeat Victimisation}

\subsection{Terminology}

Several terms describe the experience of being a crime victim more than once: \textit{revictimisation}, \textit{multiple victimisation}, \textit{repeat victimisation}, \textit{multi-victimisation}, \textit{repetitive victimisation}, \textit{recidivist victimisation}.

\begin{definition}[Repeat victim (CSEW)]
Someone who was the victim of \textbf{the same crime type more than once} in the previous 12 months (\textit{within crime type victim}).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Multiple victim (CSEW)]
Someone who experienced \textbf{more than one crime in the previous 12 months, regardless of crime type} (\textit{across crime type victim}).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Repeat victim (this study)]
Because the analysis is restricted to F\&CM, a \textbf{repeat victim} is any individual who reported, within the reference period, more than one fraud, more than one computer misuse incident, or a mix of fraud and computer misuse.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Incidence, Prevalence and Crime Concentration}

The extent of repeat victimisation can be gauged from CSEW-derived rates:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Incidence Rate (IR)}: estimated average number of incidents per 1{,}000 respondents.
    \item \textbf{Prevalence Rate (PR)}: estimated percentage of respondents who were victimised (counts victims, not incidents).
    \item \textbf{Crime Concentration (CC)}: ratio $\text{IR} / \text{PR}$ --- average number of victimisations per victim.
\end{itemize}

For most crime types, repeat and multiple victimisation imply $\text{IR} > \text{PR}$ and therefore $\text{CC} > 1$.

\section{Patterns of Repeat Victimisation in Prior Research}

The author summarises four insights from earlier RV literature, used to generate the study's hypotheses.

\subsection{A Small Share of Victims Carries Most of the Crime}

Early work showed that as much as \textbf{14\%} of the population were repeat victims and accounted for \textbf{70.9\%} of all incidents recorded on the British Crime Survey. The average number of crimes experienced by the top 10\% most victimised households rose from \textbf{57\% in 1994} to \textbf{72\% in 2012}, despite the overall drop in crime since the mid-1990s. Reducing RV can therefore prevent a large proportion of all offences from being committed.

\subsection{RV Does Not Happen by Chance}

Failed attempts to fit the distribution of RV to variations of the Poisson model indicate that RV is non-random and is associated with specific demographic characteristics. The characteristics that distinguish repeat from one-time victims tend to be the same as those that distinguish victims from non-victims (e.g., females being significantly more likely to be both one-time and repeat victims of domestic abuse).

\subsection{The `Flag' versus `Boost' Conceptualisation of Vulnerability}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Flag account}: previous victimisation \emph{indicates} pre-existing vulnerability factors that made the person a victim in the first place.
    \item \textbf{Boost account}: being victimised \emph{itself} increases vulnerability to further victimisation.
\end{itemize}

In the RV literature `vulnerability' is often equated with a higher probability of victimisation. Identifying victims as `vulnerable' on the basis of demographic characteristics alone is divisive and yields too many false positives to be useful in practice. Identifying \textbf{repeat victims} is a narrower and more precise indicator of relative vulnerability.

\subsection{Hot Spots, Hot Dots and Near-Repeats}

A key finding from environmental criminology is that high-crime areas (`hot spots') are typically distinguished not by more individuals being victimised, but by \textbf{more victims being repeatedly victimised} (`hot dots'). This has motivated `near-repeat' research, which predicts where crime will occur next on the basis of where it has previously occurred.

\textit{Limitation for F\&CM}: the spatial element underpinning near-repeats is largely irrelevant for crimes with a strong online component. Even when offline elements are involved (e.g., cold-calling combined with online banking takeover), the physical environment plays only a marginal role in victim selection. Therefore RV is likely a more useful concentration metric than near-repeats for F\&CM.

\subsection{Time-Course}

Across burglary, property crime, racial attacks, domestic violence, and computer misoffences, RV has been shown to occur \textbf{relatively soon after the first victimisation}: the risk of RV is highest immediately after victimisation.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Sample}

The data are crime reports made to Action Fraud by victims within four Welsh police forces (Dyfed/Powys, Gwent, North Wales, South Wales) between \textbf{1 October 2014 and 30 September 2016}. After de-duplication ($n = 147$ duplicates removed), the analytic sample was $n = 17{,}049$. Of these, $n = 11{,}844$ pertained to individual victims (rather than businesses or other corporate entities), divided into eight three-month periods (four quarters per year).

For the RV analysis, only reports from \textbf{three of the four forces} (Dyfed/Powys, Gwent, South Wales) were used: $n = 10{,}001$. North Wales was excluded because of high missingness in linkage variables, which skewed the linkage results.

\subsection{Collection, Linkage and Anonymisation}

Reports were collated from the monthly extracts uploaded by the National Fraud Intelligence Bureau (NFIB) onto the police's NicheRMS system and extracted by the Southern Wales Regional Organised Crime Unit (SW-ROCU). Data were processed under strict access controls within secure police premises; \textbf{de-duplicative data linkage was applied before anonymisation}, which is what made repeat-victim identification possible.

The reports were linked using a combination of \textbf{deterministic and probabilistic} record linkage, implemented with the R package \texttt{RecordLinkage}. Linkage quality was tested using \textbf{precision} and \textbf{recall (sensitivity)} on a clerical review of 100 pairs of matches; both were estimated at 100\%. The procedure was tuned to minimise false positives and may therefore have produced false negatives (missed matches).

\subsection{Limitations of Action Fraud Data}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Under-reporting of F\&CM} (only 8\% of fraud and 2\% of CM are reported to police).
    \item \textbf{Home Office Counting Rules (HOCR)} affect how RV is measured: in some categories, several incidents over time count as one crime (e.g., advance-fee fraud where the victim sends money to the fraudster repeatedly), while in others (e.g., hacking) each compromised account counts as a separate crime.
    \item \textbf{Left and right censoring}: the two-year window means earlier or later events that would constitute repeats may fall outside the observation period.
    \item \textbf{External events}: an Action Fraud institutional crisis between Q4 of 2014--2015 and Q1 of 2015--2016 sharply reduced the volume of recorded reports.
\end{itemize}

Therefore, around \textbf{8\% of crimes} reported by individual victims (and recorded) were attributable to repeat victims.

\subsection{Repeat Victim Characteristics}

\subsubsection*{Gender}

More incidents were reported by male repeat victims than by female repeat victims. The difference is statistically significant ($\chi^2(1) = 14.05$, $p < 0.01$) but the effect size is negligible (Cramér's V $= 0.03$). The odds of an incident being reported by a male repeat victim are only \textbf{1.33} times those of an incident reported by a female victim.

\subsubsection*{Ethnicity}

No significant difference was found between one-time and repeat victims in terms of ethnicity ($\chi^2(1) = 0.067$, $p > 0.5$). This result is constrained by \textbf{34\% missing values} for ethnicity and by the need to collapse to a binary White vs.\ Black and Minority Ethnic (BME) classification (small numbers in non-White categories).

\subsubsection*{Age (the largest effect)}

Repeat victims are typically \textbf{older} than one-time victims:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Repeat victims: median age \textbf{57}, mean age \textbf{53.6}.
    \item One-time victims: median age \textbf{50}, mean age \textbf{49.93}.
    \item Wilcoxon rank-sum test: $W = 3{,}197{,}004$, $p < 0.001$.
\end{itemize}

The author notes that linkage may have introduced a bias towards older victims, but date of birth (DOB) was a key linkage variable, and proxy reports on behalf of older victims (by family/friends) would, if anything, increase missingness/inaccuracy of DOB for older victims. The findings are therefore taken as indicative of \textbf{a real overlap between RV and older age}.

\begin{sourcebox}{A broader view of vulnerability}
The negative impacts of F\&CM on older victims (wellbeing, family relationships, indirect financial loss) may be associated with factors beyond the crime itself, such as social isolation or economic disadvantage. \textbf{Vulnerability should be conceptualised more broadly than the increased likelihood of (re-)victimisation alone.}
\end{sourcebox}

\subsubsection*{Proxy reports}

It had been hypothesised that reports made by a proxy on behalf of the victim would be associated with greater vulnerability and therefore with RV. The data show the \textbf{opposite}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{11\%} of one-time victims were flagged as having a proxy report.
    \item \textbf{6\%} of repeat victims had a proxy report.
    \item Difference: $\chi^2(1) = 13.58$, $p < 0.01$, Cramér's V $= 0.04$ (negligible).
\end{itemize}

This is consistent with proxy reports being associated with broader vulnerability factors that are not captured by RV measures alone.

\subsection{Time-Course of Repeat Victimisation}

The time-course analysis is limited to consecutive reports within 12 months ($n = 406$), excluding the small minority of cases ($n = 31$) with a time difference greater than 366 days.

\subsubsection*{Inter-report time}

The distribution of the time elapsed between consecutive incidents is concentrated at the lower end of the scale, with the appearance of an \textbf{exponential decrease}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{15.5\%} of all linked (repeat) incidents were reported \textbf{on the same day} ($n = 122$).
    \item Including same-day reports: mean inter-report difference $= 83$ days, median $= 12$ days.
    \item Excluding same-day reports: mean $= 115$ days, median $= 40$ days.
    \item Mean difference between first and last report by an individual repeat victim $= 137$ days, median $= 49$ days (excluding same-day).
\end{itemize}

\begin{actionboxT}{Crime prevention implication}
Interventions aimed at protecting victims from re-victimisation should ideally take place \textbf{within two weeks to one month of the first report}.
\end{actionboxT}

The exponential pattern holds across the quarters of the two-year period, although dispersion grows in year two. This is consistent with \textbf{left-censoring} (earlier in the window, fewer prior events can be observed) and with the AF crisis between Q4 (2014--2015) and Q1 (2015--2016) that depressed the volume of recorded reports.

\section{Discussion and Implications for Crime Prevention}

\subsection{Headline Findings}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Roughly \textbf{4\% of victims report 8\% of crimes}; considered separately, \textbf{3\% of fraud victims report 7\% of recorded frauds} and \textbf{6\% of CM victims report 15\% of CM crimes}. The fraud--CM gap is plausibly an artefact of HOCR.
    \item Categories with elevated RV: \textbf{advance-fee fraud} and \textbf{hacking}. This is especially striking for advance-fee fraud, since HOCR favour recording multiple fraudster-driven payments as a single crime --- in spite of which RV is still observed.
    \item A statistically significant force-level difference is driven by \textbf{Dyfed/Powys}, which has a higher proportion of repeat victims and an older typical victim profile (mean age 55, median 57 in Dyfed/Powys vs.\ 50 and 48 in Gwent and South Wales).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Counting Rules and the Direction of Bias}

Recording practices may simultaneously \textbf{over-estimate} RV for CM and \textbf{under-estimate} it for fraud:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CM (e.g., hacking)}: each compromised account/device is recorded as a separate crime, even if the incidents happen on the same day.
    \item \textbf{Fraud (e.g., romance / advance-fee)}: the modus operandi often involves building trust, escalating intimacy and repeatedly requesting money. Multiple incidents are commonly recorded together as one crime.
\end{itemize}

Even when victims report multiple crimes on the same day, there is still an opportunity to identify them as repeat victims and direct support accordingly.

\subsection{Profile of the Repeat F\&CM Victim}

The analysis suggests \textbf{male} and \textbf{older} victims are at higher risk of repeat F\&CM victimisation, while ethnicity and proxy reports show no significant association (within the limits of the data). This profile is different from typical repeat victims of violent crime and domestic violence, but is in line with the general F\&CM victim profile, supporting the view that \textit{the characteristics distinguishing repeat from one-time victims tend to mirror those distinguishing victims from non-victims}.

\subsection{Time-Course and the Two-Week--One-Month Window}

In line with prior research on burglary, racial attacks, domestic violence and computer misuse, repeat F\&CM victimisation occurs relatively soon after first victimisation. Crime-prevention activity is therefore most effective \textbf{within two weeks to one month} of the first report. The scope for intervention is, however, reduced by the \textbf{15.5\%} of repeats recorded on the same day as the preceding incident.

\subsection{The Limits of an RV Lens}

\begin{itemize}
    \item RV measurement is shaped by definitions and recording practices; both can over- or under-estimate the phenomenon.
    \item RV is a \textbf{narrower concept than vulnerability}. Whether a victim is re-victimised says little about their support needs or their ability to recover from victimisation.
    \item A framework of F\&CM vulnerability is needed that goes beyond risk of re-victimisation and accounts for victims' wider needs.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

Repeat victimisation is a complex phenomenon affected by under-reporting, recording rules and methodological constraints in linkage. Even so, this analysis demonstrates that identifying and analysing patterns of RV in reported crime produces insights of \textbf{theoretical} interest and of \textbf{practical} value to the planning and targeting of crime-prevention activities.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Identifying RV could direct limited resources towards areas where demand for crime prevention and victim support is greatest, both \textit{across} forces and \textit{within} forces (towards crime types where re-victimisation is most common and victims most vulnerable).
    \item In a context where only \textbf{15\%} of recorded crimes were `actioned' in any way (reviewed by NFIB and, where applicable, referred to a police force or partner agency for response), focusing on prevention and protecting victims from re-victimisation is possibly the most effective crime-reduction strategy available.
    \item Identifying and responding to repeat victims of F\&CM may also provide a clear and meaningful measure of law-enforcement performance.
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary}

\begin{itemize}
    \item In a two-year sample of $n = 10{,}001$ AF reports from three Welsh forces, \textbf{350 repeat victims} ($\approx 4\%$) accounted for \textbf{787 crimes} ($\approx 8\%$ of recorded individual-victim crimes).
    \item Approximately 3\% of fraud victims reported 7\% of frauds; approximately 6\% of CM victims reported 15\% of CM crimes; CM victims have $\approx 2.17\times$ the odds of being repeat victims, plausibly an HOCR artefact.
    \item \textbf{Older age} is the strongest correlate of repeat F\&CM victimisation; gender shows a small but significant male skew; ethnicity shows no significant association; proxy reports are \textit{inversely} associated with RV.
    \item The inter-report time distribution is approximately exponential (median 12 days including same-day reports, 40 days excluding); \textbf{15.5\%} of repeats occur on the same day; intervention is most effective within \textbf{two weeks to one month} of the first report.
    \item \textbf{Advance-fee fraud} and \textbf{hacking} show elevated RV; consumer and other fraud are more typical of one-time victims.
    \item Repeat F\&CM crimes are significantly more likely to be of the \textbf{same general type} as the preceding crime ($\chi^2(16) = 511.20$, $p < 0.01$, Cramér's V $= 0.54$).
    \item RV is a useful but \textbf{narrower} concept than vulnerability; an F\&CM-specific vulnerability framework is needed to address victims' wider needs.
\end{itemize}

\section{Who Are Cybercriminals? Findings from the Scientific Literature}

\subsection{From Stereotypes to Research Questions}

Popular depictions of cybercriminals revolve around a narrow stereotype: ``nerds''---bright but socially inept individuals with very high IQs and advanced technical knowledge, predominantly male, usually teenagers, never violent, and all neatly fitting one profile. Rogers (2011) argues that this image does not survive empirical scrutiny: cybercriminals exhibit \textbf{no unique characteristics} that would mark them off as a distinct, homogeneous population.

Once the stereotype is set aside, different studies on the profile of cybercriminals address different research questions, including:
\begin{itemize}
    \item What is the profile of the \textit{international} cybercriminal?
    \item Are cybercriminals intellectually superior to traditional criminals?
    \item What are their motives?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{A Framework for Analysis: The Research Process}

Profiling studies follow a general empirical-research workflow that frames the studies discussed below:
\[
\text{Choose Topic} \rightarrow \text{Focus Research Question} \rightarrow \text{Design Study} \rightarrow \text{Collect Data} \rightarrow \text{Analyse Data} \rightarrow \text{Interpret Data} \rightarrow \text{Inform Others}.
\]

\subsection{Profiling International Cyber Offenders in the US (Hadzhidimova \& Payne, 2019)}

Hadzhidimova and Payne (2019) focus on the characteristics of \textbf{international} cyber offenders prosecuted in the United States.

\paragraph{Methodology.} Data drawn from U.S.\ Department of Justice press releases between January 2009 and December 2017; sample of \textbf{225 offenders} who were citizens of a country other than the US; statistical analysis.

\paragraph{Findings.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Average age: \textbf{34.78 years}; \textbf{94\%} male.
    \item Nationality: mainly \textbf{China (26.7\%)}, \textbf{Romania (11.6\%)}, \textbf{Russia (7.1\%)}.
    \item Group offending: more than two-thirds (\textbf{68.4\%}) of offenders worked in groups.
    \item Most committed offence types: \textbf{cyber fraud} and \textbf{hacking}.
    \item Sentencing: prison sentences predominate; sentence length is significantly higher for African citizens.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cybercriminals' Motives and Drives}

\paragraph{Taylor (1999) --- six drives.} Addiction, curiosity, boredom, power, recognition, politics.

\paragraph{Rogers (2006).} Divides cybercriminals (both cyber-dependent and cyber-enabled) into \textbf{eight groups}, integrating criminological typology with motivational analysis.

\paragraph{Brewster (2015) --- hackers' motivations.} Groups hackers' motives into the following categories:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Political; ideological; commercial; emotional; informative and promotional; financial; personal; exploitation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Intellectual Capabilities: Cybercriminals vs.\ Traditional Criminals vs.\ Non-Criminals (Schiks, 2022)}

\paragraph{Research question.} Do cybercriminals have higher intellectual capabilities than traditional criminals or the general population? The study focuses on \textbf{cyber-dependent} criminals.

\paragraph{Methodology.} Data from Statistics Netherlands; comparison of \textbf{CITO test} scores across three matched groups of \textbf{143 individuals} each: cybercriminals, traditional criminals, non-criminals.

\paragraph{Results.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cybercriminals score \textbf{significantly higher} on the CITO test than traditional criminals.
    \item Cybercriminals score \textbf{significantly lower} on the CITO test than non-criminals.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keypoint}
The finding nuances the ``intellectually superior'' stereotype: cybercriminals outperform other offenders cognitively, but do not exceed the non-criminal baseline.
\end{keypoint}

\subsection{Cybercriminals and Traditional Criminals over the Life Course (Krananberg, 2018)}

Krananberg (2018) analyses \textbf{cyber-offending over the life course}, focusing on cyber-dependent crimes. The study examines the extent to which life circumstances in both private and professional life---household composition, employment, education---are differentially related to whether an individual commits cybercrime or traditional crime.

\paragraph{Methodology.} Analysis of longitudinal police data and population registration data of all suspects of cybercrime and traditional crime in the Netherlands, 2000--2012.

\paragraph{Household composition effects (similar across cyber- and traditional offending).}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cohabiting} makes an individual \textbf{less likely} to commit cybercrime than when they live alone.
    \item Compared to living alone, \textbf{single-parenthood} \textbf{increases} the likelihood of committing both cybercrime and traditional crime. Interpretation: having a partner in the household acts as a \textbf{source of informal social control}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Employment and education.} \textbf{Not} statistically significantly related to cyber-offending, whereas they reduce the likelihood of traditional offending. The protective effect of work and study against crime does not extend to cybercrime.

\subsection{The Hybrid Criminal (Hayward \& Maas, 2021)}

The figure of the \textbf{hybrid criminal} emerges from the interaction between human being and machine. Hayward and Maas (2021) propose three categories that re-frame the AI--crime relationship:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Crimes \textbf{with} artificial intelligence (AI as tool);
    \item Crimes \textbf{against} AI (AI as target);
    \item Crimes \textbf{by} AI (AI as autonomous agent).
\end{itemize}

\section{Methodological Issues}

Bada and Nurse (2021) provide a meta-analysis of the existing literature on cybercriminal profiling and identify the following methodological shortcomings, which explain why robust studies on the profile of cybercriminals are still lacking:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{No common definitions}: profiles are sometimes drawn through a general lens rather than referring to specific types of cybercriminals.
    \item \textbf{No standard approach} to studying criminal behaviour online: research focuses mainly on individual cases of cybercriminals rather than systematic comparison.
    \item \textbf{Limited research on personality traits} associated with online criminal behaviour.
    \item \textbf{No data, or great difficulty in accessing data}: a structural obstacle to large-scale empirical work.
    \item \textbf{Limited focus} (mainly on hackers); overall, perpetrators of \textbf{cyber-assisted crimes} are not studied.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Cyber-Criminal Associations}

\subsection{Cooperation Patterns Among Cybercriminals}

Compared to traditional crime, links between perpetrators of \textbf{cyber-dependent crimes} are \textbf{less likely} to result in the exchange of criminal information and in crimes committed in association. The classical criminological intuition that offenders cluster into stable groups holds only weakly online.

Nevertheless, \textbf{subcultural social ties} do exist between hackers, who treat one another as ``colleagues'' even in the absence of formal criminal associations. Three recurring examples of online criminal subcultures are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item the modern \textbf{hacker} subculture;
    \item the \textbf{prostitution client} subculture;
    \item the \textbf{extremist group} subculture.
\end{enumerate}

When cybercriminal associations do form, they remain comparatively uncommon and arise in four ways (the first two being predominant):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Exclusively \textbf{offline} social contacts.
    \item Offline social contacts \textbf{plus} an online forum used for recruitment.
    \item \textbf{Online forums combined with offline} social contacts for recruitment.
    \item Exclusively \textbf{online} forums.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Focus on the Violent Extremist Subculture (Gaudette, Scrivens \& Venkatesh, 2022)}

Gaudette, Scrivens and Venkatesh (2022), \textit{The Role of the Internet in Facilitating Violent Extremism: Insights from Former Right-Wing Extremists}, address two research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item What is the link between the Internet and violent extremism?
    \item What are the interactions between the on- and offline worlds of violent extremists?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Methodology.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Interview guide developed in consultation with stakeholders.
    \item Recruitment of \textbf{former right-wing extremists} as study participants.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Key results.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Exposure}: about \textbf{one third} had their first contact with extreme violence (EV) as a result of accessing radical right-wing content online; individuals encountered in the \textbf{offline} world also played a key role; \textbf{white-power music} was the most popular form of online content; a recurring driver was the wish ``to be part of something''.
    \item \textbf{Immersion}: the Internet \textbf{facilitates the process of radicalisation} by immersing participants in extreme right-wing content and a network of like-minded individuals; \textbf{seasoned members} play a key role.
    \item \textbf{Interactions between on- and offline worlds} occur across three dimensions:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Activities}: forums, chatrooms and social media sites are used to advertise events held in the offline world; digital platforms support recruitment and contacts at the local level.
        \item \textit{Identity}: online personas reinforce and project offline group identity.
        \item \textit{Security and surveillance}: members manage operational security across both environments.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section{Cyber-Organised Crime}

\subsection{Moral Panic or Reality?}

A central debate is whether cyber-organised crime is a documented phenomenon or a case of \textbf{moral panic}. The following observations frame the discussion:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Public and policy narratives often assume a \textbf{convergence between organised crime (OC) and cybercrime} without strong empirical evidence.
    \item Many cybercrime acts require a high degree of \textbf{organisation and specialisation}, so the level of involvement of OC groups in cybercrime is likely to be substantial.
    \item \textbf{McGuire (2012)}, based on a large sample of cases, found that \textbf{up to 80\%} of cybercrime could be the result of organised activity.
    \item This does \textbf{not} imply that the groups involved resemble traditional, hierarchical OC structures, or that they commit \textbf{exclusively} digital crime.
    \item \textbf{Wall (2015)} describes cyber-organised crime as having a \textbf{``flat'' structure} and being better characterised as an \textbf{``assembly''} rather than an organisation --- a \textit{dis-organised} model.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{McGuire's Classification}

McGuire proposes a classification of criminal groups that engage in cyber organised crime, structured by the share of online versus offline activity.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Type I --- Largely online activities}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Swarms} (e.g.\ ``hacktivist'' groups): loose, decentralised collectives mobilised around shared causes.
        \item \textbf{Hubs} (e.g.\ ``scareware'' operations): more coordinated, centred on a core that organises distributed activity.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Type II --- Both online and offline activities}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Clustered hybrids} (e.g.\ ``carding'' forums and skimmers): tightly grouped operations combining online forums with offline action.
        \item \textbf{Extended hybrids} (e.g.\ phone theft and online trading): wider networks bridging multiple physical and digital nodes.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Type III} (extension to McGuire's original two types): groups perpetrating activities that are \textbf{predominantly offline} but increasingly intersect with or are mediated through digital environments.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Further Studies: Traditional OC Online (Wang, Su \& Wang, 2021)}

A complementary research question is: \textit{how do traditional OC groups run their offline business on the Internet?} Wang, Su and Wang (2021), \textit{Organized Crime in Cyberspace: How Traditional Organized Criminal Groups Exploit the Online Peer-to-Peer Lending Market in China} (\textit{British Journal of Criminology}, 61, 303--324), examine this question through the case of traditional Chinese OC groups exploiting online \textbf{peer-to-peer (P2P) lending} platforms --- an empirical illustration of McGuire's Type II/III dynamics: offline criminal structures projecting their business model onto a digital marketplace.

\section{What Is Victimology?}

\begin{definition}[Victimology]
The \textbf{scientific study of crime victims}. The term was first used in 1947 by B.\ Mendelsohn. As a subfield of criminology, victimology studies the \textbf{causes/etiology} of victimisation, its \textbf{consequences}, and how criminal justice systems and other actors \textbf{deal with victims}.
\end{definition}

The orientation of the field has shifted over time:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Initial focus} on the \textbf{role of the victim}: notions of victim precipitation, facilitation, and provocation.
    \item \textbf{1960s --- more sympathetic view} of crime victims, driven by the victims' rights movements.
    \item Contributions of the victims' rights movements established victims as bearers of procedural and substantive rights.
    \item \textbf{Victimology today} integrates etiology, consequences, criminal-justice response and victim support.
\end{itemize}

\section{From Victimology to Cyber-Victimology}

\subsection{Unique Traits of Cyber-Victimisation}

Cybercrime victimisation has features that distinguish it from offline victimisation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item It \textbf{defies the logic of physical proximity}: offender and victim need not share a physical space.
    \item \textbf{Automation} makes it possible to victimise several persons with the same act, generating large numbers of victims at low marginal cost.
\end{itemize}

The ``human'' element and harm of cybercrime was recognised only in \textbf{2001}, by the \textbf{Council of Europe Convention on Cybercrime}.

\subsection{Typology of Cyber-Victimisation (Jaishankar, 2012/2013)}

Jaishankar (2012, 2013) proposes the following classification of cybercrimes by target:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Cybervictimisation of \textbf{governments};
    \item Cybervictimisation of \textbf{corporations};
    \item Cybervictimisation of \textbf{individuals} (interpersonal cybervictimisation);
    \item \textbf{Victimless crimes}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Measuring Cybercrime through the Victims' Lens}

\subsection{Victimisation Surveys and Cybercrime (Reep-van den Bergh \& Junger, 2018)}

Reep-van den Bergh and Junger (2018), \textit{Victims of cybercrime in Europe: a review of victim surveys} (\textit{Crime Science}, 7:5), address the question: \textit{what about cybercrime victimisation surveys?}

\paragraph{Objective.} Map existing cyber-victimisation surveys in Europe and provide estimates of \textbf{annual prevalence rates} (number of victims per population) for \textbf{six cybercrimes}.

\paragraph{Methodology.} Definition of inclusion criteria for surveys; search in databases and on the Internet, in the grey literature, and through contacts with national statistical offices.

\paragraph{Results.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{9 surveys} identified.
    \item Different annual prevalence rates depending on the crime: e.g.\ \textbf{1--3\%} for online shopping fraud; \textbf{3\%} of the population is a victim of cyberbullying.
\end{itemize}

\section{Explaining Cybercrime Victimisation}

Four theoretical perspectives are used to explain why some individuals become cyber-victims.

\subsection{Lifestyle Theory (Hindelang et al., 1978)}

Certain behaviours related to \textbf{work, school and leisure} expose individuals to risky (crime-prone) situations: \textit{certain behaviours elevate one's odds of being victimised}.

\subsection{Routine Activity Theory (RAT) (Cohen \& Felson, 1979)}

\begin{definition}[Routine Activity Theory]
Victimisation results from the convergence in time and space of three elements:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a \textbf{motivated offender};
    \item a \textbf{suitable target/victim};
    \item the \textbf{absence of capable guardianship} (humans or objects that can prevent crime from occurring).
\end{enumerate}
RAT \textit{describes} victimisation.
\end{definition}

Although Lifestyle Theory and RAT are conceptually distinct, they lead to similar hypotheses and are often combined into the \textbf{Lifestyle Routine Activity Theory (LRAT)}.

\subsection{Cyberlifestyle-Routine Activity Theory (Reyns et al., 2011)}

Reyns et al.\ (2011) revisited LRAT to adapt it to cybercrime, producing the \textbf{cyberlifestyle-routine activity theory}, which re-interprets RAT's convergence requirement for the digital environment:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Convergence in place}: offender and victim are often physically separate, yet they come together in the same \textit{virtual} space, via computer networks.
    \item \textbf{Convergence in time}: offender and victim may not interact in real time, yet they intersect in time (e.g.\ the offender sends an email that the victim reads some time later).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{General Theory of Crime (Gottfredson \& Hirschi, 1990; Schreck, 1999)}

The key factor in causing crime and deviance is \textbf{low self-control}: the inability of an individual to exercise personal restraint in the face of tempting immediate and easy gratification, both in the short and long term. The theory was developed for \textit{offending} and was \textbf{adapted to victimisation by Schreck in 1999}.

\subsection{Actor-Network Theory (van der Wagen \& Pieters, 2018)}

Actor-Network Theory (ANT) is invoked to account for emerging forms of \textbf{high-tech cybercrime} (such as ransomware, botnets and virtual theft) in which the offender targets a \textbf{hybrid victim} --- a composite of \textbf{human, technical and virtual} entities.

\section{Who Are Cyber-Victims? Findings from the Scientific Literature}

\subsection{Many Studies, Many Research Questions and Methods}

Different studies ask different research questions and use different methods to investigate cyber-victims (e.g.\ individuals vs.\ business victimisation; focus on different cybercrime types such as cyberbullying or identity theft; focus groups vs.\ surveys/interviews). Overall, the amount of research remains \textbf{limited} and findings are sometimes \textbf{conflicting}.

\subsection{A Routine-Activities Analysis (Junger et al., 2017)}

\paragraph{Aim.} Investigate the relationship between users' \textbf{routine activities} and \textbf{socio-economic characteristics} and three forms of cybercrime victimisation: \textbf{online shopping fraud}, \textbf{online banking fraud}, and \textbf{DDoS}.

\paragraph{Methodology.} Data from Eurobarometer (sample of \textbf{17{,}811 European citizens}); logistic regression.

\paragraph{Results.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Findings generally \textbf{support RAT}.
    \item Socio-economic variables (gender, age, economic factors) are \textbf{almost completely irrelevant}: the lecture summarises this finding as the \textbf{normalisation of cyber-victimisation} --- under universal connectivity, exposure ceases to be a function of socio-demographic profile.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Focusing on Repeat Cyber-Victimisation}

\subsection{Definition and Supertargets}

\begin{definition}[Repeat victimisation]
Experiencing more than one victimisation. Pease (2018) states: \textit{``People, places, products or services, vehicle, household, business, or systems (including virtual networks) are, once victimized, more likely to be victimized again. Further victimization by the same type of crime is most likely.''}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Supertargets]
The \textbf{1--2\%} of potential targets that experience around \textbf{half} of crime.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Predicting Crime: Space--Time Clustering of Residential Burglaries (UK)}

Many studies show that \textbf{previous victimisation} is a risk indicator. A line of work in the United Kingdom investigates residential burglaries to address the question: \textit{do residential burglaries cluster in time and space?}

\paragraph{Theoretical basis.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Optimal foraging theory}: animals optimise foraging strategies to maximise benefits while minimising risks and search time --- the same logic is applied to offenders selecting targets.
    \item \textbf{Transmissibility} (from epidemiology): closeness in space and time of epidemics/diseases. \textit{Does this also apply to home burglary?}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Methodology.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Georeferenced data on burglaries in \textbf{Merseyside (6 years)}.
    \item Statistical tests to verify whether space--time clustering was a random event or not.
    \item For each street, calculation of the space--time distance between break-ins.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Results.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Time of repeat victimisation} (Johnson, Bowers \& Hirschfield, 1997): the distribution of weeks between events is sharply right-skewed --- repeats concentrate in the \textbf{first weeks} after the initial event and decay over the following year.
    \item \textbf{``Neighbourhood'' effect}: in the 1-week window, the number of burglaries peaks at the victimised house and decays with the number of doors apart; the 2-week window shows a flatter but still elevated profile near the original target.
    \item \textbf{``Side of the road'' effect}: houses on the \textbf{same side} of the road as the victimised house experience more subsequent burglaries than those on the opposite side, controlling for house-number distance.
    \item \textbf{Area at risk}: houses within \textbf{400~m} of a victimised home are at increased risk for \textbf{up to one month} after the first burglary.
    \item \textbf{Prospective hotspot prediction}: predicts \textbf{62\%} of robberies that occur in the following \textbf{2 days} and \textbf{64\%} of those that occur in the following \textbf{7 days}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keypoint}
Burglary in a home is \textbf{``transmissible''}, like a disease: the immediate neighbourhood of a victimised home is at elevated risk for a bounded time window. This transmissibility logic underpins predictive policing tools that target proximate locations within short post-event windows.
\end{keypoint}

\subsection{Connection to the BJS and Correia Studies}

The repeat-victimisation logic developed in the burglary literature feeds directly into the two readings discussed elsewhere in these notes: the BJS \textit{Repeat Violent Victimization, 2005--14} (Chapter~\ref{chap:bjs}) on the offline side, and Correia (2020) \textit{Patterns of Online Repeat Victimisation} (Chapter~\ref{chap:correia}) on the online side.

\section{From Cyber-Victimisation to Prevention: Your Human Firewall}

The lecture closes on a prevention frame: cybersecurity ultimately depends on the user. The recommended viewing/discussion item is the TED talk \textit{Your Human Firewall --- The Answer to the Cyber Security Problem} by Rob May (TEDxWoking), \url{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BpdcVfq2dB8}, which argues that the most effective layer of defence against cyber-victimisation is the \textbf{human} layer --- security-aware behaviour by individual users.

\begin{sourcebox}{Reference}
Bureau of Justice Statistics, \textit{Repeat Violent Victimization, 2005--14}, Summary, NCJ 250567, August 2017. Data source: National Crime Victimization Survey (NCVS), 2005--2014.

\section{Overview and Definitions}

The BJS summary reports on the patterns of repeat nonfatal violent victimisation in the United States during the period 2005--2014, drawing on NCVS data. The reference population is persons aged 12 or older.

\begin{definition}[Nonfatal violent victimisation]
For the purposes of the report, nonfatal violent victimisation comprises four offence categories: \textbf{rape or sexual assault}, \textbf{robbery}, \textbf{aggravated assault}, and \textbf{simple assault}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Repeat violent victimisation]
A person experiences repeat violent victimisation when they suffer \textbf{two or more} violent victimisations during the year.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Headline figures (2005--2014 average):}
\begin{itemize}
    \item An average of \textbf{3.2 million persons} age 12 or older experienced one or more nonfatal violent victimisations during the year.
    \item \textbf{81\%} of victims experienced a \textbf{single} nonfatal violent victimisation.
    \item \textbf{19\%} of victims experienced \textbf{repeat} violent victimisation (two or more victimisations during the year).
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{14\%} experienced \textbf{two to five} violent victimisations.
        \item \textbf{5\%} experienced \textbf{six or more} violent victimisations.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section{Concentration of Victimisation}

A central finding is that repeat victims, although a minority, account for a disproportionate share of all violent victimisations.

\begin{itemize}
    \item In \textbf{2014}, the \textbf{19\%} of violent crime victims who were repeat victims accounted for \textbf{50\%} of all violent victimisations.
    \item The concentration was even more pronounced at the upper end of the distribution: the \textbf{5\%} of victims who experienced six or more violent victimisations accounted for \textbf{27\%} of total violent victimisations in 2014.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keypoint}
Methodological implication: a small subset of highly victimised individuals contributes a very large share of overall crime counts. Crime prevention strategies that target this subset can, in principle, achieve substantial reductions in total victimisation.
\end{keypoint}

\section{Crime Prevalence Rates: Long-Term Trends}

The report compares prevalence rates across two decades (1993 vs.\ 2014), expressed as the number of victims per 1{,}000 persons age 12 or older.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Total nonfatal violent crime prevalence rate}: decreased \textbf{62\%}, from \textbf{29.3} per 1{,}000 in 1993 to \textbf{11.1} per 1{,}000 in 2014.
    \item \textbf{Single violent victimisation prevalence rate}: decreased \textbf{60\%}, from \textbf{22.4} per 1{,}000 in 1993 to \textbf{8.9} per 1{,}000 in 2014.
    \item \textbf{Repeat violent victimisation prevalence rate}: decreased \textbf{69\%}, from \textbf{6.9} per 1{,}000 in 1993 to \textbf{2.1} per 1{,}000 in 2014.
\end{itemize}

The decline in repeat victimisation (69\%) was steeper than the decline in single victimisation (60\%) over the same period.

\section{Victim--Offender Relationship}

Patterns of repeat violent victimisation differ markedly according to the relationship between victim and offender.

\subsection{Share of Victims Experiencing Repeat Violence, by Relationship}

During 2005--2014, the percentage of victims who experienced repeat violent victimisation, broken down by the relationship to the offender, was:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intimate partner violence (IPV) victims}: \textbf{33\%}.
    \item Victims of violence committed by a \textbf{well-known or casual acquaintance}: \textbf{26\%}.
    \item Victims of violence committed by a \textbf{relative}: \textbf{25\%}.
    \item Victims of violence committed by a \textbf{stranger}: \textbf{17\%}.
\end{itemize}

IPV victims who experienced repeat violent victimisation accounted for more than \textbf{two-thirds (68\%)} of all violent crime committed against IPV victims --- the strongest concentration observed across relationship categories.

\subsection{Repetition by the Same Type of Offender}

Repeat violent crime victims were more likely to experience two or more victimisations by the \textbf{same type} of offender than by different types of offenders. Specifically:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{26\%} of IPV victims experienced repeated violence by an intimate partner.
    \item \textbf{19\%} of victims of violence committed by a well-known or casual acquaintance were victimised at least twice by a well-known or casual acquaintance.
    \item \textbf{17\%} of victims of violence committed by a relative experienced repeat relative violence.
    \item \textbf{11\%} of victims of violence committed by a stranger experienced repeat stranger violence.
\end{itemize}

\section{Types of Repeat Violence}

Repeat victimisation also varies by offence type.

\subsection{Share of Victims Experiencing Any Repeat Violent Victimisation, by Offence}

During 2005--2014:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rape or sexual assault}: \textbf{31\%} of victims experienced repeat violent victimisation.
    \item \textbf{Simple assault}: \textbf{23\%}.
    \item \textbf{Aggravated assault}: \textbf{21\%}.
    \item \textbf{Robbery}: \textbf{19\%}.
\end{itemize}

Rape or sexual assault victims experienced repeat violent victimisation at a higher rate than victims of any of the other three offence categories.

\subsection{Repetition of the Same Offence Type}

A greater percentage of rape or sexual assault victims experienced two or more victimisations of the same kind than was the case for the other offence categories:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rape or sexual assault}: \textbf{16\%} of victims experienced two or more rape or sexual assault victimisations.
    \item \textbf{Aggravated assault}: \textbf{9\%} of victims experienced repeated aggravated assault.
    \item \textbf{Robbery}: \textbf{7\%} of victims experienced repeated robbery.
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Most violent crime victims (81\%) experience a single victimisation in a given year, but the minority (19\%) who experience repeat victimisation account for half of all violent victimisations, and the very small share (5\%) experiencing six or more accounts for over a quarter of the total.
    \item All prevalence rates --- total, single, and repeat --- declined substantially between 1993 and 2014, with the steepest decline in the repeat victimisation rate ($-69\%$).
    \item Intimate partner violence shows the highest rate of repeat victimisation across relationship categories (33\%), and IPV repeat victims account for 68\% of all violence committed against IPV victims.
    \item Repeat violence tends to come from the \textbf{same type} of offender, particularly in IPV cases.
    \item Among offence types, rape or sexual assault shows the highest rates of both any repeat violent victimisation (31\%) and repeat victimisation of the same offence type (16\%).
\end{itemize}

\section{Approaches to Crime Prevention: Primary, Secondary and Tertiary}

\subsection{What Is Crime Prevention?}

\begin{definition}[Crime prevention (United Nations, 2002)]
Strategies and measures that seek to reduce the risk of crimes occurring, and their potential harmful effects on individuals and society (including fear of crime), by intervening to influence their multiple causes.
\end{definition}

In operational terms, crime prevention covers \textbf{any activity} by an individual or group (public or private) which attempts to \textbf{eliminate crime prior to it occurring or before any additional activity results}.

The dominant categorisation adopts a \textbf{public-health approach}: focus on preventing ill-health by encouraging healthy lifestyles rather than waiting for illness --- that is, \textit{investing funds early to save costs later}. By analogy, crime prevention is articulated into three levels:

\subsection{The Three Levels}

\paragraph{Primary prevention (universal).} The most universal form of crime prevention, largely undiscriminating and targeting \textbf{society as a whole}. Intended to target and \textbf{prevent criminal behaviour before it occurs}. Interventions either \textbf{reduce crime opportunities} or \textbf{enhance social factors} that reduce an individual's likelihood of becoming involved in crime. In what follows, primary prevention is analysed through \textbf{Situational Crime Prevention} and \textbf{Universal Communication Strategies}.

\paragraph{Secondary prevention (at-risk groups).} Targets population groups who have \textbf{not yet engaged} in criminal behaviour \textbf{but are at risk} of doing so (e.g.\ kids showing signs of deviant/criminal behaviour). Focus on people with increased proclivity for crime, \textbf{before they graduate into more serious offending}. Analysed through \textbf{Educational Workshops}, \textbf{Mentoring Programs}, and \textbf{Targeted Warnings and Cautions}.

\paragraph{Tertiary prevention (known offenders).} Targets individuals \textbf{after} they have become involved in crime. Focus on activities aimed at preventing those who have already engaged in criminal activity from \textbf{future reoffending}. Individuals are formally referred to programming by the criminal justice system following a criminal conviction. Analysed through \textbf{Positive Diversions} and \textbf{Restorative Justice Practices}.

\section{Primary Cyber-Crime Prevention}

\subsection{Situational Crime Prevention (SCP)}

\begin{definition}[Situational Crime Prevention (Clarke)]
SCP \textit{``comprises \textbf{opportunity-reducing measures} (1) directed at highly specific forms of crime, (2) involving the management/design/manipulation of the immediate environment in as specific and permanent way as possible, (3) so as to \textbf{increase the effort and risk} of crime and \textbf{reduce the rewards} as perceived by a wide range of offenders.''}
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Theoretical foundation.} The key assumption is that many offenders are \textbf{rational} and weigh the costs and benefits of offending. SCP builds on three theoretical strands:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the \textbf{rational choice} offender approach;
    \item \textbf{routine activities theory};
    \item \textbf{crime pattern theory}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The 25 SCP techniques.} Clarke and colleagues organise SCP interventions in a 5\,$\times$\,5 matrix along five mechanisms:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Increase the effort} (e.g.\ target harden, control access to facilities, screen exits, deflect offenders, control tools/weapons);
    \item \textbf{Increase the risks} (e.g.\ extend guardianship, assist natural surveillance, reduce anonymity, utilise place managers, strengthen formal surveillance);
    \item \textbf{Reduce the rewards} (e.g.\ conceal targets, remove targets, identify property, disrupt markets, deny benefits);
    \item \textbf{Reduce provocations} (e.g.\ reduce frustrations and stress, avoid disputes, reduce emotional arousal, neutralise peer pressure, discourage imitation);
    \item \textbf{Remove the excuses} (e.g.\ set rules, post instructions, alert conscience, assist compliance, control drugs and alcohol).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Are SCP Measures Effective in Preventing Cybercrime?}

The lecture focuses on five SCP measures translated to the cyber domain:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Antivirus software}: an effective strategy.
    \item \textbf{Warning messages}: see the factsheet \textit{What works in situational cybercrime prevention?}
    \item \textbf{Primary Crime Prevention Apps}: many typologies of apps but no rigorous evaluation; see the article \textit{Primary Crime Prevention Apps: A Typology and Scoping Review}.
    \item \textbf{Internet vigilantism}: civilian users acting as informal guardians online.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Universal Communication Strategies}

Mass-media messages (broadcast on TV or other audio/video channels) that aim to \textbf{deter people from committing crimes}. Two key primary mechanisms:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Changing perceptions of the risks} of offending.
    \item \textbf{Promoting positive behaviours and values}.
\end{enumerate}
A special additional category is the \textbf{appeal to third parties to intervene}.

\paragraph{Application to cybercrime.} Very little has been done to tackle cybercrime through universal communication strategies, with few exceptions. The flagship example is \textbf{Cyberchoices} (UK National Crime Agency), \url{https://nationalcrimeagency.gov.uk/what-we-do/crime-threats/cyber-crime/cyberchoices}.

\section{Secondary Cyber-Crime Prevention}

\subsection{Educational Workshops}

Bring together groups of (potential) offenders in \textbf{single or multiple sessions} to educate them about the nature of crime and reinforce prosocial values and behaviours. Features:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Deployed within school curricula, or selectively target \textbf{at-risk adolescents}.
    \item Combine \textbf{didactic and interactive} elements.
    \item Rely on \textbf{social-cognitive theories of learning}.
    \item Mainly aimed at \textbf{youth in primary or secondary schools} to affect \textbf{early-onset offending}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Applied to cybercrime.} Minimal empirical research to date.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{i-SAFE program} (\url{https://isafeventures.com/}). The 2006 evaluation reported: \textit{``positive and significant changes in knowledge between the treatment and comparison groups, both on average and over time. For the most part, there were no significant changes in behavior between the treatment and comparison groups.''}
    \item Arizona Attorney General's \textbf{Social Networking Safety Promotion and Cyberbullying Prevention (SNSPCP)}.
    \item \textbf{Media Heroes}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keypoint}
A recurring pattern: education programs reliably move \textbf{knowledge}, but the effect on actual \textbf{behaviour} is often null or weak.
\end{keypoint}

\subsection{Mentoring}

\begin{definition}[Mentoring]
``Commitment of time and specific efforts by a \textbf{more experienced} person to the development of a mutually beneficial, supportive and nurturing relationship with a \textbf{less experienced} person.''
\end{definition}

Mentoring offers a \textbf{corrective experience and intervention} for at-risk youth who have experienced poor relations with parents or other caregivers. The theoretical underpinnings emphasise the importance of \textbf{positive and supportive interpersonal relationships} that offer guidance to children and adolescents throughout their development.

\paragraph{Targets.} Young people who either exhibit a history of delinquent behaviour or characteristics assessed as at-risk for juvenile delinquency (individual and environmental risk factors).

\paragraph{Applied to cybercrime.} So far \textbf{no evaluation research} examining the utility of mentoring specifically for cybercrime; lessons must be drawn from studies on offline crimes.

\subsection{Targeted Warnings and Police Cautions}

Used by police to warn potential offenders with the aim of \textbf{deterring them from future offending}. Take the form of stand-alone warnings, cautions, reprimands, and instructions to ``cease and desist'', which may be delivered by law enforcement officers in person or through letters.

\paragraph{Applied to cybercrime.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item UK investigation into \textbf{Blackshades};
    \item \textbf{LizardStresser} booter service;
    \item International law-enforcement operations.
\end{itemize}
All aimed at people \textbf{already involved} in cybercrime or at least on the periphery.

\section{Tertiary Cyber-Crime Prevention}

\subsection{Positive Diversions}

Redirect individuals towards \textbf{prosocial behaviours and peer influences}; focus on \textbf{rehabilitation}. Usually targeted at young people and first-time offenders. Take a variety of forms, including art, sports, and outdoors programs.

\paragraph{Theoretical underpinnings.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Imposing traditional criminal justice sanctions on young people has \textbf{little effect on reoffending}.
    \item Positive diversions lean on \textbf{social-bond theories of crime}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Operational notes.} May serve as a \textbf{substitute} for criminal sanctions or court proceedings, or accompany them. Widely deployed in response to various low-severity crimes. Effectiveness shows \textbf{mixed findings}.

\paragraph{Applied to cybercrime.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item No empirical evaluation of positive-diversion programming for preventing cybercrime so far.
    \item It is plausible that one could \textbf{redirect the skills used for cybercrime into prosocial avenues} (e.g.\ cybersecurity).
    \item A special category transforms criminal into noncriminal behaviour: e.g.\ a young person engaging in graffiti diverted into a program encouraging legal ``urban art''. By analogy, graffiti artists and cybercriminals share a \textbf{sense of identity}, \textbf{pleasure from crime}, and \textbf{status culture}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Restorative Justice}

\begin{definition}[Restorative Justice]
A \textbf{voluntary, community-based} response to criminal behaviour that attempts to bring together the \textbf{victim}, the \textbf{offender}, and the \textbf{community} in an effort to address the harm caused by the criminal behaviour.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Theoretical link.} Strongly linked to \textbf{Braithwaite's theory of re-integrative shaming}.

\paragraph{Process.} Can occur \textbf{prior to, instead of, during, or after} the formal criminal process. Tends to take the form of \textbf{single meetings} of varying duration, chaired by a \textbf{mediator}, producing an \textbf{agreement} between the parties.

\paragraph{Applied to cybercrime.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No studies to date} specifically on restorative justice's application to cybercrime.
    \item Wealth of evidence in the offline context, with mixed findings.
    \item Application to cybercrime could help \textbf{convey a message of the harmfulness} arising from such crimes; some cybercrime victims have expressed interest.
    \item Case study: \textbf{online hate speech} (Finland) --- \url{https://yle.fi/aihe/artikkeli/2019/11/06/face-to-face-with-a-man-who-wished-an-asylum-seeker-would-kill-my-family-the}.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{What Works in Situational Cybercrime Prevention (NSCR Factsheet, 2021)}
\label{chap:scp-factsheet}
% ========================================

\begin{sourcebox}{Reference}
Asier Moneva and Jim Schiks, \textit{What Works in Situational Cybercrime Prevention?} NSCR (Nederlands Studiecentrum Criminaliteit en Rechtshandhaving) Factsheet, June 2021.
\end{sourcebox}

\section{Motivation and Scope}

For decades, \textbf{Situational Crime Prevention (SCP)} techniques have proven effective in reducing various forms of crime across many contexts, with substantial experimental support. Cybercrime researchers have sought to \textbf{extend SCP to cyberspace}. Despite the wide variety of SCP techniques, criminologists have so far applied only a few to cybercrime, evaluating:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the effectiveness of \textbf{antivirus software} in detecting malware;
    \item the ability of \textbf{monitoring software} to detect anomalous patterns of activity among employees;
    \item --- especially --- the effect of \textbf{warning banners} on cybercriminal behaviour.
\end{itemize}

\section{Situational Crime Prevention: Recap}

SCP consists of a set of \textbf{25 techniques} that reduce specific forms of crime through five mechanisms:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item increase the \textbf{effort} required to commit a crime;
    \item increase the \textbf{risk} of detection;
    \item reduce the potential \textbf{rewards} of crime;
    \item reduce \textbf{provocations} to potential offenders;
    \item eliminate \textbf{excuses} for non-compliance.
\end{enumerate}

These techniques operate \textbf{immediately before the crime occurs}, when all previous crime controls (personal and social) have failed. If situational prevention also fails, only formal crime controls (police, criminal justice system) can be invoked --- they are costly and sometimes conflict with people's rights, so it is best to avoid them when possible.

\section{Methodology of the Overview}

The factsheet focuses on \textbf{research with high validity}: studies that measure cybercrime \textbf{before and after} an intervention in experimental and \textbf{comparable control} conditions --- i.e.\ studies scoring \textbf{three or more} on the \textbf{Maryland Scientific Methods Scale}.

\begin{keypoint}
The only studies that meet this criterion are \textbf{experiments with warning banners}. All other SCP techniques applied to cybercrime have not yet been evaluated with comparable rigour.
\end{keypoint}

\section{Experiments with Online Warning Banners}

\begin{definition}[Online warning banner]
A small text frame, usually displayed to users when they try to access a system or a website. Banners work like traditional signs: placed in easily visible places to persuade the reader with a clear, short message. Criminologists use them to \textbf{increase the perceived risk} for cyber-offenders or to \textbf{remove any excuse} for non-compliance.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Experimental design.} To measure the effect of banners on human behaviour, researchers use \textbf{honeypots}: digital decoys designed to be attacked that collect data on the attack and the behaviour of the offender. Once cyber-offenders fall into the trap, they are randomly assigned to groups exposed to different stimuli --- a banner with a text (e.g.\ ``you are being watched'' or ``this is illegal''), a neutral banner (e.g.\ ``the site is loading''), or no banner at all. This allows comparison of the effect of different stimuli on similar groups of offenders to identify which one works best.

\section{Do Banners Work?}

Experiments with warning banners have mainly targeted three behaviours:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Deterring hackers from trespassing} into systems;
    \item Deterring users from consuming \textbf{barely-legal pornography};
    \item Deterring \textbf{DDoS attacks}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hacking / Trespassing}

For hacking, experiments measured the effect of banners on four outcomes: number of trespassing events, number of repeated trespassing events, duration of the trespassing, and volume of commands entered into the system.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mixed effect} on the number of trespassing and repeated trespassing events and on the number of commands entered: some banners reduced these behaviours, others had no effect.
    \item Banners appear to \textbf{significantly reduce the duration of the trespassing} --- the time spent by the offender inside the system.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Barely-Legal Pornography}

The single experiment on this topic found that banners displaying a deterrent message \textbf{halved the volume of accesses} to the website hosting barely-legal content.

\subsection{DDoS Attacks}

When banners were used to divert young people from carrying out DDoS attacks, researchers observed a change in the \textbf{increasing trend of attacks to a flat trend}.

\section{Conclusion}

Despite the growing interest in tackling cybercrime, experimental research on the effect of SCP techniques on deflecting cyber-offenders \textbf{is still in its infancy}. High-validity research has so far evaluated only a few techniques. With limited exceptions, criminologists have concentrated on \textbf{increasing the perceived risk} of detection and \textbf{removing excuses} for non-compliance --- especially through warning banners --- to deter hackers from breaking into a system.

It is not yet clear whether banners are always an effective tool for reducing cybercrime, but experiments show \textbf{promising results in specific contexts}. The factsheet calls for \textbf{more research} both on banners and on the other situational techniques that remain unexamined.

\paragraph{SCP categories already explored with warning banners.} \textit{Increase the risks} and \textit{remove the excuses}. The other three mechanisms --- \textit{increase the effort}, \textit{reduce the rewards}, and \textit{reduce provocations} --- remain largely unexplored in the cybercrime context with rigorous experimental designs.

\chapter{Primary Crime Prevention Apps: A Typology and Scoping Review (Wood, Ross \& Johns, 2021)}
\label{chap:pcp-apps}
% ========================================

\begin{sourcebox}{Reference}
Mark A.\ Wood, Stuart Ross and Diana Johns, \textit{Primary Crime Prevention Apps: A Typology and Scoping Review}, \textit{Trauma, Violence, \& Abuse}, 2021. SAGE. DOI: 10.1177/1524838020985560.
\end{sourcebox}

\section{Motivation and Aims}

Over the last decade, an array of smartphone apps has been designed to prevent crime, violence, and abuse. Their evidence base has not been analysed systematically. The article frames this as part of the \textbf{``apppification'' of crime prevention} --- the creation of apps designed to prevent crime through a variety of measures, including crime reporting, personal safety, rehabilitation and reintegration.

The review has three aims:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Establish the \textbf{extent, range, and nature} of research into smartphone apps with a primary crime-prevention function.
    \item Locate \textbf{gaps} in the primary crime-prevention app literature.
    \item Develop a \textbf{typology} of primary crime-prevention apps.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Theoretical Frame}

\subsection{Brantingham \& Faust (1976) --- Tripartite Model}

The review adopts Brantingham and Faust's tripartite model, which distinguishes \textbf{primary}, \textbf{secondary}, and \textbf{tertiary} crime prevention. Primary crime prevention apps \textit{``seek to modify or abate the social or environmental conditions that give rise to crime, or allow potential victims to manage their exposure to risky environments or circumstances.''} Secondary and tertiary apps (those that modify offender behaviour or treat conditions accompanying offending) are excluded from this review.

\subsection{Wall (2007) --- Three Levels of Smartphone Involvement}

Drawing on Wall's (2007) distinction between cyber-enabled and cyber-dependent crime, the authors distinguish three relations between a crime-prevention technique and smartphone technology:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Smartphone-enabled}: the technique can use smartphones but does not require them, and is not enhanced by being incorporated into one.
    \item \textbf{Smartphone-enhanced}: smartphones improve the speed, range, or effectiveness of the technique.
    \item \textbf{Smartphone-dependent}: the technique depends on mobile affordances and cannot exist without the device.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Mobile Affordances}

The reviewed apps draw on two affordances of smartphones:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Perpetual contact} (Mascheroni \& Vincent, 2016): the ability to communicate with others anywhere and anytime --- underpins personal safety and crime-alert apps.
    \item \textbf{Geo-tracking}: real-time locational information and crime-avoidance messaging sensitive to an individual's movements.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}

\paragraph{Methodology.} Scoping review following the \textbf{PRISMA} guidelines. Articles identified via three databases (\textbf{Web of Science}, \textbf{EBSCOhost}, \textbf{Google Scholar}), supplemented by snowballing. Search terms combined ``app(s)/smartphone application'' with terms such as ``access to justice'', ``crime'', ``crime prevention'', ``violence prevention'', ``rape prevention'', ``justice'', ``personal safety''.

\paragraph{Inclusion criteria.} (1) published \textbf{2008--2020}, (2) examined or detailed smartphone applications designed \textbf{explicitly for primary crime prevention}, (3) written in \textbf{English}. Inclusion criteria were deliberately broad to also capture engineering and computer-science \textit{software proposals} (the design phase), not only evaluations.

\paragraph{Sample.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Initially sampled: \textbf{151 papers}.
    \item Excluded: 31 (not smartphone-based, etc.); 2 (not designed for crime prevention); 57 (secondary/tertiary apps).
    \item Final corpus: \textbf{61 academic works} (34 peer-reviewed articles, 17 conference papers, 7 book chapters, 3 university research briefings).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Study-design breakdown.} Experimental ($n=3$), quantitative cross-sectional ($n=12$), qualitative ($n=9$), mixed method ($n=2$), software proposals ($n=22$), conceptual analyses ($n=12$), scoping reviews ($n=1$).

\paragraph{Analysis.} Inductive--deductive thematic analysis, coded by the three authors plus a research assistant. Articles were assessed along: \textit{app features}, \textit{study design}, \textit{crime-prevention efficacy}, \textit{advantages and disadvantages}.

\paragraph{Limitations acknowledged.} (1) Few studies systematically evaluate apps' preventive efficacy --- most describe design or critique unintended consequences. (2) The review excluded general social-media apps (Facebook, WhatsApp) sometimes repurposed for crime prevention.

\section{A Typology of Primary Crime Prevention Apps}

The review identifies \textbf{six types} of apps targeting potential victims and addressing criminogenic conditions in the physical and social environment.

\subsection{Self-Tracking Personal Safety Apps ($n = 24$)}

Aim to promote personal safety, primarily by reducing the user's risk of being victim of a violent crime. Mechanisms:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item enabling friends or family members to \textbf{track the user's location};
    \item sending an \textbf{alert} to a friend, family member or law-enforcement agency in situations where the user feels in danger;
    \item automatically sending an alert to family members after a set amount of time \textbf{if the user does not personally deactivate} it.
\end{enumerate}
Majority of literature ($n = 14$) concerns apps designed specifically for \textbf{women's safety}. Effectiveness mostly unassessed; one effective ($n = 1$), one ineffective ($n = 1$) in formal evaluations.

\paragraph{Advantages.} May increase users' feelings of safety in public space; may collect data on factors contributing to fear of crime; ``passive'' alerts can prompt alarms without requiring intentional action from the user.

\paragraph{Disadvantages.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item May reinforce \textbf{victim-blaming} attitudes by focusing on victims rather than offenders' behaviour.
    \item Apps may become redundant in light of existing smartphone affordances.
    \item Often fail to address the broader social and institutional context of violence against women, focusing only on individual factors.
    \item May be unfeasible and a safety-risk for individuals with disabled phone-sharing partners --- can be used to facilitate \textbf{stalking}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Child-Tracking Apps ($n = 3$)}

Apps designed to promote child safety through \textbf{geo-tracking of other individuals} (children and family members). Example: \textit{Life360}.

\paragraph{Concerns.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item May create a situation of over-proximity in which parents mistake control for care.
    \item May create rather than relieve parents' anxieties.
    \item May infringe on children's rights to privacy.
    \item May amplify harmful power dynamics in intimate relationships and be used to facilitate stalking.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Crime-Mapping / Alert Apps ($n = 17$)}

Apps that promote personal safety through \textbf{crowdmapping crime} and providing users with alert services and information on ``safer'' routes and locations in real time.

\begin{definition}[Crowdmapping (Quaintance, 2011)]
The aggregation of crowd-generated inputs such as text messages and social-media feeds with geographic data to provide real-time, interactive information on events.
\end{definition}

Examples:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{WikiCrimes} --- platform harnessing collective intelligence and anonymous mass collaboration for citizen crime prevention.
    \item \textit{Walksafe} --- campus safety app evaluated against Pennsylvania State University's emergency notification system.
    \item \textit{StreetSafe}, \textit{Hollaback!} --- targeted at women to combat street harassment and sexual assault.
    \item \textit{Sex Offender Tracker} --- mapped specific crime types; criticised for relying on unreliable and outdated official registries.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Disadvantages.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Often rely on subjective and racially-coded perceptions of what constitutes ``suspicious'' behaviour, leading to profiling of racial and socio-economic groups.
    \item May fuel \textbf{vigilantism}.
    \item Crime-mapping apps may draw on official databases that are historic, unreliable, or out-of-date.
    \item Striking case: \textit{SketchFactor} and \textit{GhettoTracker} (renamed \textit{Good Part of Town}) were quickly labelled racist and classist; both were taken down within days.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Decision Aid Apps ($n = 7$)}

Focus on preventing \textbf{intimate partner violence (IPV)} through personalised information and decision aids that assist individuals in making decisions based on high-quality evidence. Example: \textit{MyPlan Safety Decision App} (and trials such as \textit{Thrive}, \textit{weWomen}, \textit{ourCircle}). Notable feature: app-based interventions can be disguisable, addressing privacy concerns raised by survivors.

\subsection{Crime Reporting Apps ($n = 14$)}

Allow users to easily report various forms of crime. Unlike crime-mapping apps, reporting apps do \textbf{not} provide access to user-submitted data on reported events. Examples: \textit{Crime Stoppers}, New Orleans' \textit{Tip Submit}, \textit{Project Jagriti} (crowdsourced child abuse reporting), \textit{Project Spear} (human trafficking), \textit{iWitnessed} (guided-recall to improve witness accounts).

\paragraph{Open issue.} Such apps must comply with data-protection legislation (e.g.\ EU GDPR); Charitou et al.\ (2018) showed via \textit{TRILLION} that security-by-design can reconcile compliance with operational efficacy.

\subsection{Education Apps ($n = 4$)}

Provide users with \textbf{uniform, generic} educational material about recognising specific forms of crime. Unlike decision-aid apps, they do not deliver individually tailored messages or plans. Examples:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{SAP\_MobAPP} --- primary-school children, teaches recognition of sexually-abusive behaviour/situations.
    \item \textit{Thrive} --- aimed at mothers who have experienced IPV.
    \item Mendel \& Sharapov's (2020) content analysis of 63 ``anti-trafficking apps'' found that \textbf{75\%} had fewer than 100 downloads, \textbf{17\%} had non-functional features, and many spread \textbf{misinformation} about human trafficking.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keypoint}
Pfaller (2017) / \v{Z}i\v{z}ek's concept of \textbf{interpassivity} is invoked: rather than educating users about people trafficking, such apps may produce \textit{different forms of ignorance about it} --- ``ignorance facilitates an interpassivity where it is believed that the apps are acting in an individual or organization's place.''
\end{keypoint}

\section{Discussion}

The crime-prevention landscape is shifting: where prevention used to be a mediated, top-down process (police, government, councils), users now choose from a marketplace of mobile apps. Some are developed by traditional crime-prevention providers, others by specialist app developers or individual entrepreneurs. Consumers need informed advice on \textbf{what they need} and \textbf{how to use it}.

\paragraph{Two key gaps.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Large gaps in product documentation and evidence} for many apps currently available. App stores' technical and content reviews are limited; inaccurate or low-quality content is common.
    \item \textbf{Near-absence of systematic evaluations} of app outcomes --- especially for ``personal safety'' apps. No studies to date have rigorously examined the efficacy of women's personal safety apps, crime-mapping apps, child abuse prevention apps, or generic personal safety apps in meeting their primary aim: \textit{reducing victimisation from crime}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Limited interdisciplinarity.} Only seven articles in the review were published in criminology journals or books; crime prevention app development is substantially driven by software-engineering considerations with limited dialogue between computational and criminological knowledge.

\paragraph{Critical social-science perspective.} Social scientists have predominantly concerned themselves with the \textbf{unintended consequences} of these apps:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Apps like \textit{Circle of 6} aim to \textit{``limit or alter the behaviour of potential victims''} rather than perpetrators.
    \item Crime prevention apps have been read as \textbf{responsibilisation} strategies, transferring risk management to individuals (Comack \& Peter, 2005; Gray, 2009; Mowlabocus, 2018).
    \item ``Responsibilisation has been \textit{transcoded} into the language and logic of the smartphone'' (Manovich, 2001).
\end{itemize}

\section{Implications for Practice, Policy and Research}

The article's headline implications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item To date, \textbf{no studies} have evaluated the efficacy of child-abuse prevention apps, crime-tracking/mapping apps, or crime-reporting apps. \textit{Recent enthusiasm over these forms of victim-facing app-based crime prevention interventions is not supported by adequate research or evaluation evidence}.
    \item Numerous studies highlight the need for \textbf{evaluative research} that considers potential \textbf{unintended consequences} of such apps (e.g.\ increasing users' fear of crime or victim-blaming sentiments).
    \item Few software proposals ground their designs in \textbf{crime-prevention theory} or evidence: greater dialogue needed between computational and criminological knowledge.
    \item In the absence of systematic effectiveness reviews, users need advice on choosing an app appropriate to their needs and a realistic appraisal of the \textbf{likely benefits and risks} (including privacy and data-security risks) of using them.
    \item Crime-prevention agencies should carry out \textbf{systematic user reviews} of apps and provide ``\textbf{curated}'' apps that offer useful prevention assistance without compromising users' safety or security.
\end{itemize}

Rosa Maria Vadal\`a, \textit{The Law Cyber-Revolution's Challenges and the Main International Legal Sources}, lecture slides, ``Cybercrime'' course (Cybercriminal law and criminal procedure at the national and international level), M.Sc.\ in Data Science and Artificial Intelligence, University of Verona, A.Y.\ 2025/2026. This part of the course shifts from criminology to \textbf{law}. The lecturer organises the Vadal\`a unit into four blocks: (1) the origin and evolution of international and European cybercriminal law (including the EU AI Act); (2) the criminal liability of Internet Service Providers in the light of the case law of the Court of Justice of the EU; (3) the most important cybercrime offences of Italian legislation; (4) cybercriminal procedure law and technological investigations. This chapter covers block (1) at the \textbf{international} level.

\section{The Cyber-Revolution and Its Challenges to Law}

Since the Internet was opened to the public (around \textbf{1994}), the ``cyber-revolution'' has transformed the conditions under which crime is committed, with two structural consequences for criminal law:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the appearance of \textbf{new varieties of criminal activity} (e.g.\ illegal access) \emph{and} an increase in the incidence of common crimes (terrorism, defamation, money laundering);
    \item the \textbf{borderless, global dimension} of this new or increasing criminal activity.
\end{itemize}

Further effects identified in the lecture:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the central \textbf{role of ISPs} in the flow of information between users and recipients;
    \item users can be \textbf{simultaneously victims and perpetrators} of abuses and offences;
    \item the rise of \textbf{organised cybercriminals} and of \textbf{cybercrime-as-a-service};
    \item a \textbf{crisis of dogmatic categories} (act, event) and of the criteria for attributing responsibility (causality and the culpability principle);
    \item the double face of \textbf{Artificial Intelligence}, which generates new and common crimes but is also a tool to prevent and investigate them.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keypoint}
The guiding principle of the whole field is: \textbf{``what is illegal offline cannot be legal online''}, even if it occurs in new ways and forms. From this follow three needs: (i) \textbf{amending or creating} substantive and procedural criminal-law norms; (ii) international and European \textbf{harmonisation} of those norms; and (iii) international and European \textbf{cooperation} between law-enforcement agencies.
\end{keypoint}

\subsection{Computer Crimes vs.\ Cybercrimes}

A recurring terminological distinction frames the entire unit:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Computer crimes / IT crimes}: crimes that include, among their \textbf{essential constituent elements}, an express reference to technical-IT elements without which they would not even be conceivable.
    \item \textbf{Cybercrimes}: a broader, flexible category covering both crimes \emph{committed in cyberspace} and traditional crimes that \emph{can also} be perpetrated by and with computers or networks.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Evolution of Cyber Criminal Law}

The lecture traces a chronological line of instruments:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Council of Europe}: Recommendations of \textbf{1989}; the \textbf{Budapest Convention} on Cybercrime (2001); the \textbf{Lanzarote Convention} on the protection of children (2007).
    \item \textbf{European Union directives}: e-commerce (2000); child pornography (2011); cyber-attacks (2013); NIS (2016); the Cybersecurity Act (2019); fraud and counterfeiting of non-cash means of payment (2017).
    \item \textbf{The next step}: the \textbf{United Nations Convention against Cybercrime}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Council of Europe: Recommendation No.\ R (89) 9}

The Council of Europe Recommendation \textbf{No.\ R (89) 9} on computer-related crime (with the final report of the European Committee on Crime Problems) proposed two lists of offences for national legislatures.

\begin{sidebox}{The two lists of Recommendation R (89) 9}
\textbf{Minimal list (8 offences):} (a) computer-related fraud; (b) computer forgery; (c) damage to computer data or programs; (d) computer sabotage; (e) unauthorised access; (f) unauthorised interception; (g) unauthorised reproduction of a protected computer program; (h) unauthorised reproduction of a topography.

\textbf{Optional list (4 offences):} (a) alteration of computer data or programs; (b) computer espionage; (c) unauthorised use of a computer; (d) unauthorised use of a protected computer program.
\end{sidebox}

\section{The Budapest Convention on Cybercrime (2001)}

The Council of Europe \textbf{Convention on Cybercrime} (Budapest, 23 November 2001) is the first binding international treaty on the subject. It is described as ``more than a legal document'': a \textbf{framework} that lets practitioners from the Parties share experience and cooperate in concrete cases, including emergencies, beyond its specific provisions.

\subsection{Objectives and Structure}

\begin{definition}[The three objectives of the Budapest Convention]
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Harmonising} the domestic substantive criminal law of cybercrime offences and connected provisions;
    \item providing for the domestic \textbf{procedural} powers needed to investigate and prosecute such offences (and any offence committed by means of a computer system, or whose evidence is in electronic form);
    \item setting up a fast and effective regime of \textbf{international cooperation}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The Convention is divided into four chapters: (I) use of terms; (II) measures at domestic level (substantive and procedural law); (III) international cooperation; (IV) final clauses.

\textbf{Article 1} defines the key terms used throughout the field: \emph{computer system} (any device or interconnected group of devices that, pursuant to a program, performs automatic data processing), \emph{computer data}, \emph{service provider}, and \emph{traffic data} (data on a communication's origin, destination, route, time, date, size, duration or type of service).

\subsection{The Substantive Offences (Articles 2--13)}

Section 1 of Chapter II organises substantive criminal law into \textbf{five titles} and nine offences:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Title 1 --- CIA offences} (against the \textbf{confidentiality, integrity and availability} of computer data and systems), \textbf{Arts.\ 2--6};
    \item \textbf{Title 2 --- computer-related offences} (the ``classic'' ones), \textbf{Arts.\ 7--8};
    \item \textbf{Title 3 --- content-related offences} (child pornography), \textbf{Art.\ 9};
    \item \textbf{Title 4 --- copyright} and related-rights infringements, \textbf{Art.\ 10};
    \item \textbf{Title 5 --- ancillary liability and sanctions}, \textbf{Arts.\ 11--13}.
\end{itemize}

All offences must be committed \textbf{``intentionally and without right''}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Art.\ 2 --- Illegal access}: access to the whole or any part of a computer system.
    \item \textbf{Art.\ 3 --- Illegal interception}: interception by technical means of non-public transmissions of computer data (including electromagnetic emissions).
    \item \textbf{Art.\ 4 --- Data interference}: damaging, deletion, deterioration, alteration or suppression of computer data.
    \item \textbf{Art.\ 5 --- System interference}: serious hindering of the functioning of a computer system.
    \item \textbf{Art.\ 6 --- Misuse of devices}: production, sale, procurement, import, distribution or making available of a device (including a program) or of access codes/passwords designed for committing Arts.\ 2--5, and the \emph{possession} of such items with the relevant intent (a preparatory offence).
    \item \textbf{Art.\ 7 --- Computer-related forgery}: input, alteration, deletion or suppression of data producing inauthentic data, with intent that it be acted upon as authentic.
    \item \textbf{Art.\ 8 --- Computer-related fraud}: causing loss of property to another by data manipulation or system interference, with fraudulent or dishonest intent to procure an economic benefit.
    \item \textbf{Art.\ 9 --- Child pornography}: producing, offering/making available, distributing/transmitting, procuring and possessing child pornography through a computer system (the definition covers a real minor, a person appearing to be a minor, and realistic images representing a minor).
    \item \textbf{Art.\ 10 --- Copyright}: infringements committed wilfully, on a commercial scale and by computer system, defined by reference to the \textbf{Paris Act 1971} revising the \textbf{Berne Convention}, \textbf{TRIPS}, the \textbf{WIPO} treaties and the \textbf{Rome Convention}.
\end{itemize}
Title 5 adds \textbf{Art.\ 11} (attempt and aiding/abetting), \textbf{Art.\ 12} (corporate liability) and \textbf{Art.\ 13} (sanctions and measures).

\subsection{Procedural Powers and International Cooperation}

Section 2 of Chapter II provides procedural powers whose scope (\textbf{Art.\ 14(2)}) extends to: (a) the offences of Arts.\ 2--11; (b) any other offence committed by means of a computer system; and (c) the \textbf{collection of evidence in electronic form} of any criminal offence. The same wide scope governs international cooperation (\textbf{Art.\ 23}, opening Chapter III).

\begin{sidebox}{Additional Protocols to the Budapest Convention}
The Convention is supplemented by a \textbf{First Additional Protocol} on the criminalisation of acts of a \textbf{racist and xenophobic} nature committed through computer systems (\textbf{CETS 189}, 2003), and a \textbf{Second Additional Protocol} on enhanced cooperation and the disclosure of electronic evidence (EU Member States are authorised to ratify it under \textbf{Council Decision 2023/436}). Implementation is supported by the \textbf{C-PROC} (Cybercrime Programme Office) and the \textbf{T-CY} (Cybercrime Convention Committee).
\end{sidebox}

\section{The Lanzarote Convention (2007)}

The Council of Europe \textbf{Convention on the Protection of Children against Sexual Exploitation and Sexual Abuse} (Lanzarote, 25 October 2007) has two objectives --- to \textbf{prevent and combat} the sexual exploitation and abuse of children, and to \textbf{protect} the rights of child victims --- pursued through a \textbf{``4 Ps'' approach}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prevention} (Arts.\ 4--10);
    \item \textbf{Protection} (Arts.\ 11--17);
    \item \textbf{Prosecution} (Arts.\ 18--37);
    \item \textbf{Promotion} of national and international cooperation (Arts.\ 38--44).
\end{itemize}

The substantive offences (Arts.\ 18--23) include \textbf{sexual abuse} (Art.\ 18), \textbf{child prostitution} (Art.\ 19), \textbf{child pornography} (Art.\ 20 --- whose six criminalised acts notably include \emph{knowingly obtaining access} through ICT), \textbf{participation of a child in pornographic performances} (Art.\ 21), \textbf{corruption of children} (Art.\ 22) and, most innovatively, the \textbf{solicitation of children for sexual purposes} (\textbf{Art.\ 23, ``child grooming''}): the intentional ICT-mediated proposal by an adult to meet a child for the purpose of committing abuse, \emph{where the proposal is followed by material acts leading to such a meeting}.

\section{The United Nations Convention against Cybercrime}

The \textbf{UN Convention against Cybercrime} (long title: \textit{Strengthening International Cooperation for Combating Certain Crimes Committed by Means of Information and Communications Technology Systems and for the Sharing of Evidence in Electronic Form of Serious Crimes}) is presented as the next, broader stage of international harmonisation.

\subsection{A Broader Model}

While the Budapest Convention focuses on \textbf{core cyber-dependent offences} (illegal access, data and system interference, computer fraud and forgery), the UN Convention keeps these but \textbf{expands} the field:
\begin{itemize}
    \item it modernises terminology --- \textbf{``ICT systems''} replaces ``computer systems'' and \textbf{``electronic data''} (Art.\ 2) replaces ``computer data'';
    \item it introduces explicit definitions of \textbf{content data}, \textbf{personal data} and \textbf{serious crime} (an offence punishable by a maximum deprivation of liberty of \textbf{at least four years});
    \item it adds financial/economic notions: \textbf{property} (including \emph{virtual assets}), \textbf{proceeds of crime}, \textbf{freezing/seizure}, \textbf{confiscation} and \textbf{predicate offence};
    \item it criminalises \textbf{laundering} of proceeds linked to offences in Arts.\ 7--16 and the \textbf{non-consensual dissemination of intimate images} (\textbf{Art.\ 16}), while \textbf{omitting} the copyright-infringement offence found in Budapest;
    \item it strengthens \textbf{prevention} and the protection of \textbf{critical information infrastructure} (\textbf{Art.\ 21} requires aggravating circumstances; examples: energy, financial networks, healthcare, government services), and adds confiscation of proceeds and witness/victim protection to procedural measures.
\end{itemize}

\begin{actionbox}
\textbf{Criticisms and risks.} Safeguards depend heavily on \textbf{domestic law}, creating a risk of inconsistent human-rights protection. The \textbf{coexistence} with the Budapest Convention may impose overlapping obligations on States and raises the danger of \textbf{fragmentation} of cybercrime regulation; the effectiveness of both treaties depends on international cooperation, harmonised implementation and respect for human-rights standards.
\end{actionbox}

\section{Legal Basis: Article 83 TFEU}

EU competence to legislate in this field rests on the \textbf{Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU)}.

\begin{definition}[Article 83(1) TFEU]
The European Parliament and the Council may, by \textbf{directives} adopted under the \textbf{ordinary legislative procedure}, establish \textbf{minimum rules concerning the definition of criminal offences and sanctions} in areas of \textbf{particularly serious crime with a cross-border dimension}. The listed areas are: terrorism; trafficking in human beings and sexual exploitation of women and children; illicit drug trafficking; illicit arms trafficking; money laundering; corruption; counterfeiting of means of payment; \textbf{computer crime}; and organised crime.
\end{definition}

Under \textbf{Article 83(2)}, where the approximation of criminal laws proves essential to ensure the effective implementation of a harmonised Union policy, directives may likewise establish minimum rules on offences and sanctions in the area concerned (the example given is the protection of the environment).

\section{The EU Approach to Cybercrime}

According to the European Commission, ``cybercrime consists of criminal acts committed online by using electronic communications networks and information systems.'' It is a \textbf{borderless} issue classified in three broad definitions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Crimes specific to the Internet} --- e.g.\ attacks against information systems, phishing;
    \item \textbf{Online fraud and forgery} --- large-scale fraud through identity theft, phishing, spam, malicious code;
    \item \textbf{Illegal online content} --- child sexual abuse material, incitement to racial hatred or terrorism, racism and xenophobia.
\end{enumerate}
The EU response combines directives that \textbf{tackle large-scale cyber-attacks} (2013 attacks on information systems; 2019 non-cash payment fraud), directives that \textbf{address new online developments} (2011 child sexual exploitation), and a broader \textbf{cybersecurity} framework.

\section{Directive 2011/93/EU --- Child Sexual Abuse and Exploitation}

\textbf{Directive 2011/93/EU} (13 December 2011), replacing Framework Decision 2004/68/JHA, combats the sexual abuse and exploitation of children and child pornography.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Definitions (Art.\ 2)}: a \textbf{``child''} is any person below \textbf{18}; the \emph{age of sexual consent} is set by national law. Child pornography covers material depicting a real child, a person \emph{appearing} to be a child, or \emph{realistic images}, in sexually explicit conduct.
    \item \textbf{Offences (Art.\ 5)} with minimum maximum penalties: acquisition/possession and \emph{knowingly obtaining access} via ICT (\textbf{at least 1 year}); distribution/dissemination/transmission and offering/supplying/making available (\textbf{at least 2 years}); \textbf{production} (\textbf{at least 3 years}).
    \item \textbf{Grooming (Art.\ 6)}: the ICT-mediated proposal by an adult to meet a child below the age of consent, \emph{followed by material acts} (at least 1 year); also covers attempts to solicit a child to provide self-produced material.
    \item \textbf{Investigation (Art.\ 15)}: effective investigative tools (as for organised/serious crime) and victim identification, in particular by \emph{analysing} child-abuse material.
    \item \textbf{Jurisdiction (Art.\ 17)}: territory or nationality of the offender, extended to offences committed via ICT \emph{accessed from} the Member State's territory.
    \item \textbf{Art.\ 21} targets advertising of abuse opportunities and \emph{child sex tourism}; \textbf{Art.\ 25} requires the \textbf{prompt removal} of hosted child-abuse web pages and allows \textbf{blocking access} to such pages.
\end{itemize}

\section{Directive 2013/40/EU --- Attacks against Information Systems}

\textbf{Directive 2013/40/EU} (12 August 2013), replacing Framework Decision 2005/222/JHA, is the principal EU instrument specifically on cybercrime.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Definitions}: an \emph{information system} is a device (or group of devices) that automatically processes computer data, plus the data itself; \emph{without right} means conduct not authorised by the right-holder or not permitted under national law.
    \item \textbf{Offences} are aligned with the Budapest Convention: \textbf{Art.\ 3} illegal access (by infringing a security measure; cf.\ Art.\ 2 Budapest); \textbf{Art.\ 4} illegal system interference (cf.\ Art.\ 5); \textbf{Art.\ 5} illegal data interference (cf.\ Art.\ 4); \textbf{Art.\ 6} illegal interception (cf.\ Art.\ 3); \textbf{Art.\ 7} tools for committing offences (cf.\ Art.\ 6). Notably, \textbf{mere possession is not criminalised}.
    \item \textbf{Penalties}: the offences carry a maximum of \textbf{at least 2 years}; data/system interference reach \textbf{at least 3 years} where a significant number of systems is affected via a tool, and \textbf{at least 5 years} where committed within a \textbf{criminal organisation}, causing \textbf{serious damage}, or against a \textbf{critical infrastructure} information system.
    \item \textbf{Jurisdiction (Art.\ 12)}: where the offender is physically present on the territory, or where the targeted system is on the territory.
\end{itemize}
Recital 16 clarifies that the ``tools'' offence targets malware (including software able to create \textbf{botnets}); a \textbf{direct intent} requirement avoids criminalising tools produced for legitimate purposes (e.g.\ security testing), as does Recital 17 for unauthorised-access situations lacking criminal intent.

\section{The Cybersecurity Framework}

A parallel strand protects networks and systems rather than punishing offenders:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{NIS Directive (Directive (EU) 2016/1148)}: national strategies, a \textbf{Cooperation Group}, a \textbf{CSIRTs network}, and security/notification obligations for \textbf{operators of essential services} and \textbf{digital service providers} (online marketplaces, search engines, cloud services). Italian implementation: \emph{d.lgs.}\ 65/2018, with administrative fines for omitted security measures or notifications.
    \item \textbf{Cybersecurity Act (Regulation (EU) 2019/881)}: gives \textbf{ENISA} a permanent mandate and creates a framework for European \textbf{cybersecurity certification schemes} for ICT products, services and processes.
    \item \textbf{NIS 2 Directive (Directive (EU) 2022/2555)}: expands the scope to new sectors and entities, improving resilience and incident-response capacities; it repeals the 2016 NIS Directive and points forward to the Cyber Resilience Act and the Cyber Solidarity Act.
    \item \textbf{Italian national cybersecurity perimeter}: Decree-law 105/2019 (criminal offences for wilful non-compliance, imprisonment 1--3 years), PCM decrees 131/2020 and 81/2021, Decree-law 82/2021 (establishing the \textbf{National Cybersecurity Agency}), and Decree-law 21/2022 (extension to 5G and cloud).
\end{itemize}

\section{Data Protection Sources}

Cybercrime law intersects with data-protection law:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{GDPR (Regulation (EU) 2016/679)} states the principles of lawful, fair and transparent processing; purpose limitation; data minimisation; storage limitation; \textbf{integrity and confidentiality} (appropriate security against unauthorised processing, loss or damage); and \textbf{accountability}.
    \item the \textbf{Law Enforcement Directive (Directive (EU) 2016/680)} governs the processing of personal data by competent authorities for the prevention, investigation, detection or prosecution of offences, with parallel principles of lawfulness, purpose limitation, adequacy, accuracy, storage limitation and security.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Artificial Intelligence Act}

The \textbf{Regulation laying down harmonised rules on Artificial Intelligence (AI Act)} adopts a \textbf{risk-based approach} that consciously mirrors the GDPR (risk assessment and mitigation, compliance-monitoring systems, supervisory authorities, liability and penalties).

\begin{definition}[AI system (AI Act)]
A \textbf{machine-based system} designed to operate with \textbf{varying levels of autonomy}, that may exhibit \textbf{adaptiveness} after deployment, and that, for explicit or implicit objectives, \textbf{infers}, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as \textbf{predictions, content, recommendations or decisions} that can influence physical or virtual environments.
\end{definition}

The Regulation lays down rules on market surveillance, \textbf{harmonised transparency} rules (for systems intended to interact with people, emotion-recognition and biometric-categorisation systems, and systems that generate or manipulate content), \textbf{specific requirements for high-risk systems}, and \textbf{prohibitions} of certain practices. \textbf{High-risk} systems (safety components or Annex III use-cases) must be capable of being \textbf{effectively overseen by natural persons} (human oversight). Transparency duties require that people be \textbf{informed} they are interacting with an AI system and that synthetic audio/image/video/text be \textbf{marked as artificially generated}.

\begin{sidebox}{The AI Act and criminal law}
\textbf{Direct relevance (Arts.\ 5--6 and Annex III).} Among the \textbf{prohibited} practices, with narrow law-enforcement exceptions, are: AI systems that \textbf{assess or predict the risk of a person committing an offence based solely on profiling or personality traits} (except to support a human assessment grounded in objective, verifiable facts); and \textbf{real-time remote biometric identification} in publicly accessible spaces for law-enforcement purposes (except for targeted searches of specific victims, prevention of specific threats including terrorism, or the search for suspects of specific offences).

\textbf{Indirect relevance (Art.\ 9).} Criminal liability may arise for the \textbf{negligent use} of AI systems in otherwise lawful activities.

The European Parliament's \textbf{resolution of 6 October 2021} had already called for a ban on AI \textbf{mass-scale scoring} of individuals and on the use of AI to \textbf{propose judicial decisions}, and warned about bias, discrimination and the need for human oversight.
\end{sidebox}

\subsection{AI as an Enabler of Crime}

The criminogenic side of AI is illustrated by the Europol report on \textbf{ChatGPT and Large Language Models} (2023). Drawing on workshops of the \textbf{Europol Innovation Lab}, it warns that generative models already make it \textbf{easier than ever} for criminals with little technical knowledge to act --- e.g.\ to protect their identity online, to draft convincing fraudulent or phishing text, and to assist with malicious code. (The public version of the report is partly \textbf{redacted}; its concrete use-case examples are not disclosed.) Its three recommendations for law enforcement are to \textbf{understand} the technology, \textbf{raise awareness} of its dangers, and \textbf{engage with stakeholders} to ensure safe use. This complements the EPRS observation that AI lowers a classic phishing tell (poor spelling) and enables \textbf{deepfakes} used to fabricate ``evidence'' for scams.

Principal sources: Directive 2000/31/EC (e-Commerce); the Digital Services Act; and the case law of the Court of Justice of the EU (CJEU) and the European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR).

\section{Three Models of Liability for Third-Party Content}

How far an intermediary is liable for content generated by its users varies across legal systems:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the \textbf{strict-liability model} (e.g.\ Thailand, China);
    \item the \textbf{safe-harbour model} (European Union);
    \item the \textbf{broad-immunity model} (United States).
\end{itemize}

\section{The EU Safe-Harbour Regime: Directive 2000/31/EC}

The \textbf{e-Commerce Directive} (Directive 2000/31/EC, 8 June 2000) establishes three conditional exemptions for information-society service providers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Art.\ 12 --- ``Mere conduit''}: no liability for transmitted information provided the provider \emph{does not initiate} the transmission, \emph{does not select the receiver}, and \emph{does not select or modify} the information.
    \item \textbf{Art.\ 13 --- ``Caching''}: no liability for automatic, intermediate and temporary storage that makes onward transmission more efficient, provided the provider does not modify the information, complies with access and updating conditions, does not interfere with lawful use of technology to obtain usage data, and \emph{acts expeditiously to remove} cached information once it learns the source has been removed or a court/authority has ordered removal.
    \item \textbf{Art.\ 14 --- Hosting}: no liability for information stored at a recipient's request provided the provider has \emph{no actual knowledge} of illegal activity (nor awareness of facts making it apparent) or, \emph{upon obtaining such knowledge, acts expeditiously} to remove or disable access.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keypoint}
\textbf{Art.\ 15 --- No general obligation to monitor.} Member States may \textbf{not} impose on providers a general duty to monitor the information they transmit or store, nor a general duty to actively seek facts indicating illegal activity. They \emph{may}, however, require providers to inform authorities of alleged illegal activities or to identify recipients with whom they have storage agreements.
\end{keypoint}

\section{The CJEU on ``Passive'' Hosting}

The hosting exemption applies only to a \textbf{passive} host that has not played an active role giving it knowledge of, or control over, the stored data:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{L'Or\'eal v eBay} (C-324/09, Grand Chamber, 12 July 2011): an online-marketplace operator plays an \emph{active} role --- losing the Art.\ 14 exemption --- when it \textbf{optimises the presentation} of the offers for sale or \textbf{promotes} them; even a passive host cannot rely on the exemption if a \textbf{diligent economic operator} should have realised the offers were unlawful and it failed to act.
    \item \textbf{Peterson v Google/YouTube and Elsevier v Cyando} (C-682/18 and C-683/18, Grand Chamber, 22 June 2021): video- and file-sharing platforms fall within Art.\ 14 provided they do not play an active role; to lose the exemption the operator must have \textbf{knowledge or awareness of specific illegal acts}. Such platforms do not themselves make a \textbf{``communication to the public''} of protected content \emph{unless} they go beyond merely providing the platform --- e.g.\ by having specific knowledge and failing to act, by not putting in place expected technological measures, or by selecting/providing tools for, or knowingly promoting, illegal sharing.
\end{itemize}

\section{Injunctions and Removal Powers}

Despite the absence of a general monitoring duty, several instruments empower authorities to order removal or cooperation: Recital 45 and \textbf{Art.\ 18} of the e-Commerce Directive (interim measures to terminate an infringement), \textbf{Arts.\ 18--20} of the Budapest Convention (orders to submit subscriber information and to collect traffic data in real time), and \textbf{Art.\ 11} of Directive 2004/48/EC on the enforcement of IP rights (injunctions against intermediaries). The CJEU has drawn the boundary between permissible \emph{targeted} measures and prohibited \emph{general} ones:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SABAM v Netlog} (C-360/10, 16 February 2012): a court may \textbf{not} order a hosting provider to install a filtering system applied \emph{indiscriminately} to all users, as a \emph{preventive} measure, \emph{at its own expense} and for an \emph{unlimited period}.
    \item \textbf{McFadden v Sony Music} (C-484/14, 15 September 2016): an injunction requiring an access provider to prevent copyright infringement is \textbf{legitimate} where the provider may choose the technical measure --- even if limited to \textbf{password-protecting} the connection, provided users must reveal their identity.
    \item \textbf{Glawischnig-Piesczek v Facebook Ireland} (C-18/18, 3 October 2019): Art.\ 15 does \textbf{not preclude} ordering a host to remove information \textbf{identical} (regardless of who stored it) or \textbf{equivalent} to content previously declared unlawful, provided the monitoring is limited to essentially unchanged messages requiring no independent assessment; removal may even be ordered \textbf{worldwide} within the framework of international law.
\end{itemize}

\section{ISP Cooperation and Data Retention}

A major line of case law limits ISP-assisted surveillance by reference to the \textbf{Charter of Fundamental Rights} --- \textbf{Art.\ 7} (private life and communications), \textbf{Art.\ 8} (personal data, subject to control by an \emph{independent authority}) and \textbf{Art.\ 52} (limitations must be provided by law, respect the essence of rights, and be \emph{necessary} and \emph{proportionate}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Digital Rights Ireland} (C-293/12 and C-594/12, 8 April 2014): the Data Retention Directive 2006/24/EC is \textbf{invalid}; the fight against serious crime does not by itself justify general, undifferentiated retention, and the directive lacked clear, precise limits ``strictly necessary'' to circumscribe the interference.
    \item \textbf{Tele2 Sverige} (C-203/15, C-698/15, 21 December 2016): access must be restricted to \emph{fighting serious crime}, subject to \emph{prior review} by a court or independent authority, with data retained within the EU. \textbf{Ministerio Fiscal} (C-207/16, 2 October 2018): access merely to identify the owners of SIM cards is \emph{not sufficiently serious} an interference to require a serious-crime limitation.
    \item \textbf{La Quadrature du Net} (C-511/18, C-512/18, C-520/18, 6 October 2020): general retention is permitted only \emph{for national security}, against a \emph{genuine, present or foreseeable} serious threat, subject to effective review and limited in time.
    \item \textbf{Prokuratuur} (C-746/18, 2 March 2021): access to traffic/location data allowing precise conclusions about private life must be \emph{confined to combating serious crime}, and cannot be authorised by the \textbf{public prosecutor's office} (which directs the investigation and is not a neutral party).
\end{itemize}

\section{Fundamental Rights: Search Engines and User Comments}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Google Spain} (C-131/12, 13 May 2014): a search engine's indexing is \textbf{``processing'' of personal data} and its operator is the \textbf{``controller''}; the data subject may, as a rule, require that information \textbf{no longer be linked} to their name (the \textbf{``right to be forgotten''}), unless a preponderant public interest (e.g.\ the data subject's \emph{role in public life}) justifies access.
    \item \textbf{ECtHR} case law on liability for user comments: \textbf{Delfi AS v.\ Estonia} (2015) upheld liability of a commercial news portal for extreme \emph{hate-speech} comments it failed to remove promptly; \textbf{MTE \& Index.hu v.\ Hungary} (2016) found a \textbf{violation of Art.\ 10} where domestic courts had not properly balanced freedom of expression; see also \textbf{Pihl v.\ Sweden} (2017) and \textbf{H\o iness v.\ Norway} (2019).
\end{itemize}

\section{The Digital Services Act}

The \textbf{Digital Services Act (DSA)}, a Regulation amending Directive 2000/31/EC, preserves the conduit/caching/hosting structure (Arts.\ 4--6), the \textbf{no-general-monitoring} rule (Art.\ 8) and orders to act against illegal content or to provide information (Arts.\ 9--10), but adds substantial new duties:
\begin{itemize}
    \item dedicated \textbf{notice-and-action} mechanisms and notification duties for hosting providers (Arts.\ 16--18);
    \item \textbf{safeguards} when user content is removed (statement of reasons, complaint mechanisms, out-of-court dispute resolution);
    \item higher \textbf{transparency and accountability} on content moderation, advertising and algorithms;
    \item additional obligations for \textbf{very large online platforms} to manage systemic risks (with independent audits), and an exclusion for micro/small enterprises.
\end{itemize}
Further measures include tracing of online sellers, a \textbf{crisis-response mechanism}, protection of minors, a \textbf{ban on targeted advertising by profiling children} or using special-category data, a ban on \textbf{``dark patterns''}, and data access for researchers. Oversight is centralised: the \textbf{European Commission} is the primary regulator for very large platforms and search engines (reaching 45 million users), supported by the \textbf{European Centre for Algorithmic Transparency}.

Italian statutes discussed: Law 547/1993 and Law 48/2008 (ratifying the Budapest Convention).

\section{The Substantive Framework (Picotti)}

Before the specific offences, Picotti situates the field within the \textbf{cybernetic revolution}, defined by two characteristics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{automation} --- processing by programs and algorithms increasingly ``capable of self-learning and correcting autonomously'';
    \item \textbf{hyper-connectivity} --- the speed of accumulation, processing and exchange of data, producing \emph{big data}.
\end{itemize}
Both characteristics are now embedded in the legal definitions of \emph{computer system} and \emph{computer data} (Art.\ 1 Budapest Convention; Art.\ 2 Directive 2013/40/EU).

\subsection{From Computer-Crime to Cybercrime}

Picotti charts the conceptual transition from \textbf{computer-crime in the strict sense} (offences whose essential constituent elements expressly reference technical-IT elements) to the broader, \emph{flexible} category of \textbf{cybercrime} (any crime that can be committed \emph{in} cyberspace). The path runs from the CoE Recommendation R (89) 9 (1989) through the AIDP Rio Congress (1994, chaired by U.\ Sieber) to the Budapest Convention (2001) and its Additional Protocol on racism and xenophobia (ETS 189, 2003).

\subsection{New Legal Interests}

Two \textbf{new fundamental legal interests} emerge that criminal law must protect:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{IT confidentiality} --- the right to an \emph{exclusive information space} free from third-party intrusion, distinct from data protection (recognised in CoE Convention 108/1981, Art.\ 8 of the EU Charter, and the German Constitutional Court's ``right to the confidentiality and integrity of IT systems'', 27 February 2008; linked to the \emph{right to be forgotten}, \textit{Google Spain}, and Art.\ 17 GDPR);
    \item \textbf{IT security / cybersecurity} --- a condition for criminalising illegal access (Art.\ 3 Directive 2013/40/EU; \textbf{Art.\ 615-ter} of the Italian Criminal Code) that has also become the object of \emph{obligations} (GDPR Arts.\ 25, 31, 35; Directive 2016/1148; Regulation 2019/881; the Italian national cyber-security perimeter), now a \textbf{non-alienable public interest}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keypoint}
Cybercrime strains the traditional \textbf{dogmatic categories} of criminal law: it affects \textbf{causality} and the imputation of \emph{actus reus}/\emph{mens rea} to multiple actors (creators, users, ISPs, AI). Picotti uses the distinction between the \textbf{formal perfection} of a crime (when its minimal constituent elements exist) and its \textbf{exhaustion} (when its harmful potential is fully expended) to analyse the protracted \textbf{``cybernetic event''} --- e.g.\ online defamation, hate speech or revenge porn, which keep spreading beyond the offender's control (cf.\ Cass.\ no.\ 54946/2016, \emph{Taverchio}). The recurring principle --- ``what is illegal offline cannot be legal online'' --- rejects the ``Code is Law'' idea (Lessig) as a substitute for democratically legitimate criminal law.
\end{keypoint}

\section{The Two Pillar Statutes}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Law 547/1993} (``Amendments and Additions to the Criminal Code and the Code of Criminal Procedure on Cybercrime''), drafted by the \textbf{Vassalli Commission}, is the \textbf{cornerstone} of Italian computer-crime law. It both created new offences and amended existing ones, inserting in particular \textbf{Arts.\ 615-ter, 615-quater, 615-quinquies, 640-ter, 491-bis, 635-bis} and \textbf{Arts.\ 617-quater/quinquies/sexies}.
    \item \textbf{Law 48/2008} ratified the \textbf{2001 Budapest Convention} (which Italy had signed ``among the first''). Comprising 14 articles in four chapters, it integrated the Criminal Code and Code of Criminal Procedure --- notably reorganising the \textbf{damage} offences into Arts.\ 635-bis to 635-quinquies and introducing \textbf{computer forensics} into procedural law.
\end{itemize}

\section{Offences against Confidentiality, Integrity and Availability}

\subsection{The ``Computer Domicile'': Unauthorised Access (Art.\ 615-ter)}

\textbf{Art.\ 615-ter} (unauthorised access to a computer or telecommunication system) is placed among the crimes against the \textbf{inviolability of domicile} --- a new ``\textbf{computer domicile}'' --- linked to Art.\ 14 of the Constitution. It punishes two conducts: \emph{abusively breaking into} a system protected by security measures, and \emph{remaining} in such a system against the express or tacit will of the entitled person.

\begin{sidebox}{Key interpretive points (Art.\ 615-ter)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Security measures} need not be sophisticated: even a simple password or access code suffices, since what matters is that it \emph{expresses the holder's will to reserve access} --- the protection need not be effective.
    \item \textbf{Access} is complete the moment the offender overcomes the protective barriers; \emph{effective knowledge} of the data is not required. An \textbf{attempt} is configurable where the offender tries but fails to bypass protections.
    \item Penalty: \textbf{imprisonment 1 to 5 years}. The offence is prosecutable \emph{ex officio} (rather than on complaint) in aggravated forms: e.g.\ committed by a public officer or system operator abusing their role, with violence on persons/things, or causing destruction/damage to the system or its data.
\end{itemize}
\end{sidebox}

\subsection{Related CIA Offences}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Art.\ 615-quater} --- abusive possession and diffusion of \textbf{access codes}: a ``prodromal'' offence to Art.\ 615-ter, requiring the specific aim of profit or of causing harm; covers acquiring codes (including biometric credentials), communicating them, or supplying instructions to violate a protected system.
    \item \textbf{Art.\ 615-quinquies} --- diffusion of programs/devices aimed at damaging or disrupting a system. Reshaped by Law 48/2008 (modelled on Art.\ 6 Budapest, ``misuse of devices''), it shifted from a \textbf{generic-intent} to a \textbf{specific-intent} crime: mere awareness of dissemination is no longer enough; the offender must know the harming characteristics of the item.
    \item \textbf{Arts.\ 617-quater / 617-quinquies / 617-sexies} --- interception, prevention or interruption of computer/telematic communications, installation of interception devices, and falsification/alteration/suppression of their content. The conduct's defining feature is \textbf{fraudulence} (covert modality); the protected interest is the \textbf{security of the system} (genuineness + confidentiality).
    \item \textbf{Art.\ 616} --- extended to electronic correspondence (e-mail, instant messaging), punishing acquaintance with, subtraction, or suppression of others' closed correspondence.
    \item \textbf{Art.\ 491-bis} --- computer forgery: the rules on public/private documents apply to a public or private \textbf{computer record} (the original, support-bound definition of ``computer record'' was removed in 2016).
\end{itemize}

\section{Damage Offences (Arts.\ 635-bis to 635-quinquies)}

Originally a single offence (Art.\ 635-bis), the damage regime was split by Law 48/2008 to mirror Arts.\ 4--5 Budapest (data interference vs.\ system interference):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{635-bis} --- damage to information, data and programs (now prosecutable \emph{on complaint}, except in aggravated forms);
    \item \textbf{635-ter} --- damage to data used by the State, a public body or public utility (\emph{ex officio}, given data sensitivity);
    \item \textbf{635-quater} --- damage to computer or telecommunication systems;
    \item \textbf{635-quinquies} --- damage to \textbf{public-utility} systems.
\end{itemize}
Law 48/2008 added two aggravating circumstances to all damage offences: commission with \textbf{violence to the person or threat}, and \textbf{abuse of the role of system operator}. The latter targets those who exploit their \emph{specific} knowledge of the violated system (passwords, structure, data organisation) --- whether \emph{operator}, \emph{programmer}, \emph{systems analyst} or \emph{analyst}.

\section{Computer Fraud (Art.\ 640-ter)}

\textbf{Art.\ 640-ter} (computer fraud) punishes obtaining an unjust profit, to another's detriment, through the \textbf{manipulation of a computer system} (altering its functioning against the lawful holder's intentions) --- even without the artifices required by ordinary fraud (Art.\ 640), the ``objective deceptive adequateness'' lying in the manipulation itself. A reform of 2013 (Decree-law 93/2013, converted by Law 119/2013) added an aggravated hypothesis where the offence is committed \textbf{with theft or undue use of the digital identity} (imprisonment 2 to 6 years and a fine).

\section{Offences against the Person and Content-Related Offences}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cyberstalking (Art.\ 612-bis)} --- stalking was introduced by Law 38/2009; Decree-law 93/2013 added the \textbf{aggravating circumstance} of the ``fact committed through computer or telematic tools''. Cyberstalking is not a stand-alone ``computer crime'' but a \emph{free-conduct} habitual offence against psychic freedom; the aggravation requires genuine \textbf{amplification} of the harmful message (e.g.\ massive, periodic or automated sending), not a single e-mail or message. The victim may seek a police \textbf{warning} (\emph{ammonimento}) and protective measures (prohibition of approaching, ban on communication).
    \item \textbf{Cyberbullying (Law 71/2017)} --- the first Italian legislative definition (prompted by the \emph{Carolina Picchio} case) targets conduct meant to \textbf{isolate a minor}. A victim aged at least \textbf{14} (or their parents) may demand obscuring/removal/blocking of harmful data from the website operator within \textbf{48 hours}, failing which the \textbf{Data Protection Authority} intervenes within a further 48 hours; the \emph{ammonimento} procedure is extended to cyberbullying, keeping minors \emph{outside the penal circuit} where possible. Applicable crimes include insult (594), defamation (595) and threat (612).
    \item \textbf{Identity theft} --- not a stand-alone offence; covered chiefly by \textbf{Art.\ 494} (substitution of person), in concurrence with the CIA offences (615-ter/quater/quinquies), fraud (640) and computer fraud (640-ter), plus the digital-identity aggravation of Art.\ 640-ter. The case law applies Art.\ 494 to creating false social-media or e-mail profiles using another's image or data.
    \item \textbf{Grooming online (Art.\ 609-undecies)} --- introduced by \textbf{Law 172/2012} implementing the Lanzarote Convention. It punishes \emph{soliciting} a minor under \textbf{16} to commit listed sexual offences, ``solicitation'' being any act to gain the child's trust through \textbf{artifices, flattery or threats}, including via technology. Unlike Art.\ 23 Lanzarote, the Italian provision \textbf{lowers the punishment threshold}: it does not require a meeting proposal or material acts, punishing grooming already in the trust-building phase --- a \textbf{specific-intent} crime of indirect danger that demands rigorous judicial scrutiny of the offender's purpose under the principle of offensiveness.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Cybercriminal Procedure Law and Technological Investigations}
\label{chap:procedure}
% ========================================

\begin{sourcebox}{Reference}
G.\ Ziccardi, \textit{Cyber Law in Italy}, 3rd ed., Part VII ``Computer-Related Crime'', ch.\ 3 ``Application of Criminal Procedure Law in the Area of ICT'' (P.\ Perri; \S 7 on hacking tools by G.\ Ziccardi). Sources discussed: Italian Code of Criminal Procedure (\emph{c.p.p.}) as amended by Law 547/1993 and Law 48/2008; the Budapest Convention; Law 269/1998; the Orlando Reform (Law 103/2017) and Legislative Decree 216/2017.
\end{sourcebox}

The fourth block of the Vadal\`a unit turns from \emph{what} is punished to \emph{how} cybercrime is investigated and proven. Italian procedural law has been adapted incrementally, often by stretching traditional institutions rather than creating new ones, around three themes: \textbf{interception} of telematic communications, the correct acquisition of \textbf{digital evidence} (computer forensics), and \textbf{covert online operations}.

\section{Adapting the Code of Criminal Procedure}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Law 547/1993} (Arts.\ 11--13) inserted \textbf{Art.\ 266-bis c.p.p.} (``interception of computer and telematic communications'') and amended Art.\ 268 on the execution of interceptions.
    \item \textbf{Law 48/2008}, implementing the Budapest Convention, brought \textbf{computer forensics} into the Code by amending \textbf{Arts.\ 244, 247, 352 and 354 c.p.p.}: inspections, searches and seizures of computer systems (even protected ones) must now adopt \textbf{technical measures ensuring the preservation of the original data and preventing its alteration}, and provide, where possible, a \textbf{bit-stream image copy} of the data.
\end{itemize}

\section{Interception of Telematic Communications}

\textbf{Art.\ 266-bis c.p.p.} allows interception of the flow of communications between computer/telecommunication systems in proceedings concerning crimes committed via such technologies (and the crimes for which ordinary interception under Art.\ 266 is admitted --- e.g.\ serious offences against the Public Administration, drugs, weapons, child pornography under Art.\ 600-ter).

\begin{sidebox}{Procedure for interception (Art.\ 266 regime)}
The public prosecutor requests authorisation from the \textbf{judge for preliminary investigations (GIP)}; in urgent cases the prosecutor may order interception by motivated decree, to be \textbf{validated within 48 hours}. Interception may last \textbf{up to 15 days}, renewable by the judge for further 15-day periods.
\end{sidebox}

\section{Computer Forensics and Digital Evidence}

\begin{definition}[Computer forensics]
The discipline that combines law and computer science to \textbf{collect and analyse data} from computer systems, networks, wireless communications and storage devices in a way that is \textbf{admissible as evidence} in court. ``Digital evidence'' is information stored or transmitted in digital form that has probative value.
\end{definition}

Developed in the United States from the 1980s (the FBI's \textbf{CART}, Computer Analysis and Response Team), the discipline is organised into \textbf{network}, \textbf{computer} (single device), \textbf{electronic} and \textbf{mobile} forensics, and is performed \emph{post mortem} (after a ``computer accident''). Its central guarantee is the \textbf{chain of custody}: procedures ensuring the integrity and authenticity of the evidence from identification, through collection and analysis, to production in court. The four canonical steps are \textbf{identification}, \textbf{storage}, \textbf{analysis} and \textbf{exhibition}; a result obtained outside a scientifically acceptable, Code-compliant procedure risks \textbf{inadmissibility}.

\begin{keypoint}
The technical guarantee of integrity is the \textbf{bit-stream copy} --- a bit-by-bit copy of the support (including hidden, deleted or overwritten data) --- verified through a \textbf{hash algorithm} (\textbf{MD5}, now considered vulnerable, and \textbf{SHA-1}), which produces a \emph{digest} that must be identical for the original and the copy. \textbf{Write blockers} inhibit any write operation on the original. Forensic analysis should prefer \textbf{open-source tools} (e.g.\ The Sleuth Kit, Autopsy, alongside EnCase and the police-developed \emph{POLCAT}) for \textbf{transparency} and the right of defence; where modification is unavoidable, the procedure of \textbf{Art.\ 360 c.p.p.} (guarantees for unrepeatable technical ascertainments) applies.
\end{keypoint}

Acquisition technique depends on whether the system is on or off and on urgency, giving \textbf{four modalities} --- from laboratory acquisition of a powered-off, non-urgent system, through the \textbf{``cold boot''} of an urgent powered-off system (booting from a trusted external device after recording BIOS time/date), to the careful, ordered capture of \textbf{volatile data} from a running system that must first be isolated (``freezing''), where full \emph{repeatability} may not be attainable.

\section{Contrasting Online Child Sexual Exploitation}

\textbf{Article 14 of Law 269/1998} authorises special covert operations to investigate child-exploitation offences (Arts.\ 600-bis, 600-ter, 600-quinquies c.p.):
\begin{itemize}
    \item criminal-police officers (not ordinary agents), within operations ordered by senior officials and authorised by the judiciary, may carry out \textbf{simulated purchases} of pornographic material and related intermediation;
    \item the \textbf{Postal and Communications Police}, on reasoned request of the judiciary, may use \textbf{cover identities}, activate \textbf{decoy sites} (``honeypots'') and create/manage areas of communication or exchange to trace offenders.
\end{itemize}
These operations raise the controversial figure of the \textbf{``provoker agent''} (a form of instigation): the majority view excludes liability (justified by duty, Art.\ 51 c.p.), but the Court of Cassation has held (judgment 39706/2003) that this special regime cannot be applied by analogy beyond the cases of Art.\ 14. A 2006 reform added Arts.\ 14-bis to 14-quinquies of Law 269/1998: a \textbf{National Centre} against online child pornography, \textbf{reporting and data-retention duties} for providers (at least 45 days), \textbf{filtering} obligations for connectivity providers, and \textbf{financial measures} (reporting/contract rescission by banks and intermediaries).

\section{Trojan Horses (``Captatori Informatici'')}

The most contested investigative tool is the \textbf{trojan horse} (\emph{captatore informatico}): malware covertly installed on a connected device that can capture all data traffic, remotely activate the microphone and camera, search the disk and copy it, and log keystrokes.

\begin{itemize}
    \item The debate crystallised in \textbf{2016--2017} around the Court of Cassation, United Sections, \textbf{``Scurato'' decision (no.\ 26889/2016)} and the \textbf{Orlando Reform (Law 103/2017)}, implemented by \textbf{Legislative Decree 216/2017}; \textbf{Law 3/2019} later extended the tool to \textbf{corruption} cases.
    \item For the most serious crimes (Art.\ 51, paras.\ 3-bis and 3-quater c.p.) the device may always be activated; otherwise, in private dwellings (Art.\ 614 c.p.) only where there is a well-founded reason to believe a crime is taking place.
\end{itemize}

\begin{actionbox}
\textbf{Safeguards and unresolved tensions.} The reform requires that microphone activation occur only by a \textbf{specific remote command} within the judge's authorisation; that the \textbf{GIP} give heightened reasons for the necessity of the method; that recordings be transferred only to the \textbf{prosecutor's server} to guarantee integrity; and that the trojan be \textbf{deactivated and made permanently unusable} at the end. Results are usable only for the authorised crimes (and, elsewhere, only if indispensable for offences under Art.\ 380 c.p.p.). The core criticism remains that the sensor is ``genetically'' designed to intercept \emph{any} information about \emph{anyone} near the device --- a structural threat to the privacy of unrelated third parties --- and that its use in private places, even absent proof of an ongoing crime, stretches the architecture of the Code.
\end{actionbox}
